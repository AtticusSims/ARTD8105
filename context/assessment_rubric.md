## The short version

| What | Weight |
|---|---|
| Class participation | 10% |
| **Case Study — the reading stream** | **30%** |
| **Project — the practice output** | **40%** |
| Term Paper | 20% |

These weights come from the approved course document and cannot be changed. Three things follow from them, and all three are worth knowing on day one.

**The practice output is the largest single component.** Forty per cent sits on the thing you make, its process journal, its documentation and its exhibition. This is a practice doctorate and the weighting says so.

**The reading stream is worth more than the paper.** Thirty per cent against twenty. That will look strange if you assume reading is preparation for writing. It is not: in this course the reading stream *is* a body of work — twenty reading notes, two or three venue files, and one deep precedent analysis. Treat it as a component you are building, not as homework you are getting through, and it becomes the easiest thirty per cent in the course. Neglect it and it is very hard to recover, because it cannot be produced in the last fortnight.

**The paper at twenty per cent is still the hardest thing you will do.** The weight does not reflect the workload, and I would rather say that plainly than let you discover it in November.

**Read the three components as one piece of work, not three.** The case study is where your bibliography comes from — every reading note you write is a source the paper will cite, and the deep precedent analysis is the related-work section in draft. It is also what tells you what your project should be: you cannot know what would count as a contribution until you have seen what the field already accepts. So the thirty per cent for the case study is not thirty per cent *instead of* the paper; it is the thirty per cent that makes the paper and the project possible. If you do the reading stream properly, you will find in November that a large part of both of the other components is already written.

There is no examination.

## The scale

Everything is scored on a four-point scale. **2.75 is the threshold** — the level at which work meets doctoral expectations.

| | |
|---|---|
| **4** | Exemplary — could be shown to another student as a model |
| **3** | Strong — meets doctoral expectations with evident command |
| **2** | Developing — present, but underpowered or inconsistent |
| **1** | Insufficient — absent, or contradicted by your own evidence |

# A · Case Study — the reading stream (30%)

*This component is the engine for the other two. The notes you write here become the paper's bibliography and its related-work section; the precedents you analyse are what tell you whether your project is doing something the field has not already absorbed. Assessed on its own terms below, but never think of it as a separate task.*

## A1 · Reading breadth and currency

Two streams: the shared canon (sessions 1–8, discussed in class) and your personal stream (all term, converging on your own topic from session 6).

- **4** — Twenty or more reading notes. Your personal stream visibly converges and reaches primary sources, not only surveys. At least three works from outside the Anglophone canon.
- **3** — Sixteen or more notes; convergence evident.
- **2** — Notes present but sporadic, or the personal stream never converges on anything.
- **1** — Fewer than ten notes, or notes that summarise without engaging.

## A2 · Critical engagement

The *Critical Thinking* and *Creative Thinking* sections of your reading notes.

- **4** — You identify limitations the paper does not concede. You question the authors' assumptions. You propose a genuine alternative.
- **3** — You consistently identify real limitations rather than generic ones.
- **2** — Critique is present but generic — "more testing needed," "small sample size."
- **1** — Summary only.

> Generic critique scores 2. Name something the paper itself does not admit, or do not write the section.

## A3 · Working out what your target venue actually wants

*Taught in session 6.*

Every journal and conference has standards for what it will publish, and almost none of them write those standards down. You will learn a method for reconstructing them from public evidence: the venue's own instructions to its reviewers, plus close reading of several papers it recently accepted. You write up what you find in a short document — one per venue you are considering.

Then you test it. Take a paper the venue accepted that you did **not** use to build your version of the standard, and score it. If your version would have rejected a paper they published, your version is wrong and needs redoing.

- **4** — Done for two or more venues, with the standard written as specific claims someone else could check. Tested against a held-out paper, and the result reported honestly — **including a first attempt that failed and had to be redone.**
- **3** — Done for at least one venue, covering both the rules the venue states and the ones you had to infer, and tested.
- **2** — You collected the venue's stated rules, but the part you had to infer is thin, or you never tested it.
- **1** — You named a venue but did not investigate it.

## A4 · One thing, studied properly

A close analysis of a single work, practitioner or paper that your own project is responding to. It becomes the backbone of the section in your paper where you explain what already exists and where your work departs from it.

- **4** — Positions the precedent against your own project with a stated point of departure.
- **3** — Thorough, with a clear relation to your work.
- **2** — Descriptive; the relation to your own work is asserted rather than argued.
- **1** — Not attempted, or a summary.

# B · Project — practice, process, documentation (40%)

## B1 · The artefact

- **4** — It works, it can be interrogated, and it could not have been made without the method you claim. Physical output where the project warrants it.
- **3** — It works and demonstrates the capability you claim.
- **2** — Partial, or works only under demonstration conditions.
- **1** — Does not function, or does not actually require the method claimed.

## B2 · Your process journal, kept as evidence

The journal is not a diary and not a progress report. It is where you record what actually happened while you worked, in enough detail that you can reason from it later. In session 10 you will read your own journal back and find your paper's central finding in it — which only works if there is something there to find.

- **4** — Records decisions with the alternatives you rejected, failures with a diagnosis, surprises, and numbers. Legible to a stranger. Analysable — and actually analysed in session 10.
- **3** — Consistent recording of decisions and failures, specific enough to be evidence.
- **2** — Kept, but narrative or affective rather than evidential.
- **1** — Reconstructed near the deadline, or absent.

> "I struggled with this for days" tells a reader nothing. *What* you struggled against, and what the resistance revealed, tells them a great deal.

## B3 · Documentation

- **4** — Documentation functions as evidence: consistent conditions, scale present, process as well as outcome, video where the work is temporal. Someone who never sees the object can assess its properties.
- **3** — Complete and usable.
- **2** — Outcome documented; process not.
- **1** — Insufficient for anyone to judge the work.

## B4 · Exhibition

**The Faculty group exhibition is a required part of this course.** Every course in the Faculty takes part, and your work is expected in it. The show is held after the end of the teaching term; dates and venue are confirmed by the Faculty and will be announced during the semester.

Two things are assessed. At the final critique on **25 November** your work must be finished and ready to show, with its documentation and install requirements specified. Then the work must actually appear in the exhibition.

- **4** — Exhibited with real care for how the work is encountered. Install specified and documented well enough that someone else could set it up without you in the room.
- **3** — Exhibited and legible; documentation complete.
- **2** — Exhibited but under-considered, or install and documentation left improvised.
- **1** — Not exhibited.

> Because the show falls after the last class, plan for it in October rather than the week before. Anything that needs a plinth, a power supply, a wall, a projector or a dark room needs to be known well in advance — bring it up when exhibition planning opens in session 11.

# C · Term Paper (20%)

## C1 · Your contribution

Not what you made — what is now known because you made it. In session 6 you will learn the handful of forms this can take, and you declare which one you are claiming; each needs a different kind of evidence.

- **4** — A claim someone could argue with, true of more than your own project, with the form of contribution declared and the right evidence supplied for it.
- **3** — A clear claim, adequately evidenced.
- **2** — A claim that is descriptive, unfalsifiable, or not generalised.
- **1** — Reports activity. "First I did X, then Y, then Z."

## C2 · Positioning

- **4** — Related work builds the gap your paper fills. Every major work is critiqued, not listed.
- **3** — Related work is argued rather than listed.
- **2** — Adequate coverage, list-like.
- **1** — Thin or absent; novelty asserted without support.

## C3 · Method account

- **4** — Explains how you worked **and how you analysed what you recorded** to reach your finding. A stranger could follow it.
- **3** — Process explained clearly.
- **2** — Process described, but not connected to how the findings were derived.
- **1** — Absent — the paper jumps from artefact to conclusion.

> This is the section where inexperienced papers give themselves away. Professionals explain how they worked and how they analysed it; beginners skip straight to the conclusion.

## C4 · Fit to your target venue

- **4** — Meets every hard requirement — length, format, anonymity, references — and visibly follows the standard you worked out for that venue in A3.
- **3** — Meets the venue's stated requirements.
- **2** — Requirements met loosely; format or length off.
- **1** — Not submittable as presented.

## C5 · Scholarly integrity

- **4** — Full bibliography audit; every reference resolved; limitations stated precisely; AI disclosure written from your actual log.
- **3** — References verified; limitations stated; disclosure present.
- **2** — Some unverified references, or limitations stated perfunctorily.
- **1** — Unverifiable references present.

> **A fabricated citation caps this criterion at 1 regardless of how good the rest of the paper is.** We do the bibliography audit together in class on 18 November. Two of the three "required readings" in this course's own accreditation document turned out not to exist — that is what happens when nobody checks.

# D · AI Process Literacy

Because AI assistance is required, how you handle it is assessed — across every component, not as a separate mark.

## Disclosure is a precondition, not a criterion

Your submission must state the tool, the version, the date, and the task. *"We used AI for some things"* does not meet it. **If disclosure is missing, or your documentation contradicts your artefacts, the whole dimension scores 1.**

## D1 · Can you show what the AI did?

*Set up in session 2.*

You keep a running record of your work with the agent — what you asked, what it produced, what you did with it. You do not write it by hand: you start each session with a short instruction and the agent writes the entries while you work. You review a summary once a week and correct anything wrong.

- **4** — You can point to any part of your work and say whether it was AI-assisted and how, backed by a record written at the time and matched by your saved version history.
- **3** — Record kept through the term with weekly reviews; you can account for all the major pieces of work.
- **2** — Record exists but with substantial gaps, or written up after the fact.
- **1** — No usable record.

## D2 · Did you turn things down?

When you reject something substantial the agent suggested — a direction, a framing, a method — you write a short note saying what was proposed, what you did instead, and why. Expect one to three of these in a busy week.

- **4** — Your notes show real rejections with reasons, and the reasons show your judgement developing over the term. Sometimes the path you did not take is better documented than the one you did.
- **3** — Regular notes showing genuine choosing rather than accepting.
- **2** — Some rejection is visible, but barely recorded, or only about small things.
- **1** — No evidence of rejection. Everything the agent produced was used.

> If you have written no such notes by session 6, either you are accepting everything the agent gives you, or you are not recording the moments when you don't. Both are problems, and both are fixable in October and not in November.

## D3 · Do you treat prompting as design work?

Beyond individual prompts, you can give the agent standing background — a document describing your project, or what you worked out about your target venue — so it reasons from your material rather than from generic assumptions. Writing those documents is design work, and it is assessed as such.

- **4** — You wrote your own background documents and improved them over the term. You can explain why you structured a prompt the way you did, and what you changed when it did not work.
- **3** — Your prompts clearly developed rather than being improvised each time; you wrote and used a venue document.
- **2** — Prompting works but you never think about why.
- **1** — One-shot prompting; no background documents written.

## D4 · Authorship — verified orally

Checked in tutorial and at the 11 November presentation, not through a written form.

You may be asked to explain any line of submitted code, any sentence of your manuscript, or to identify which material was AI-assisted.

- **4** — You can explain any part on demand, including code you did not type. You identify AI-assisted material accurately and without defensiveness, and you can say which parts you could not rebuild alone.
- **3** — You can explain all major components.
- **2** — You can explain the work generally, but not specific passages.
- **1** — You cannot explain submitted material. *This is an academic integrity matter, not a grading one.*

### On fairness

Oral verification advantages students who are fluent and confident in English, and this cohort is not first-language English. So: **questions are given to you in writing in advance. You may answer in Chinese.** The check is spread across the term rather than concentrated in one high-stakes moment. Hesitancy is never treated as evidence of a problem — if you can explain your work slowly, you can explain your work.

# What is *not* assessed

Stated plainly so you don't optimise for the wrong things.

**Whether your paper is accepted anywhere.** Submission-ready by 25 November is the requirement. Acceptance happens outside this semester and outside this grade.

**Whether you actually submit it.** Strongly encouraged and fully supported — but the deadlines, costs and timelines are not within your control, so they are not marked.

**Co-authorship with the instructor.** It may be offered where the contribution warrants it, it will only be discussed after grades are submitted, and it is entirely optional. **Your grade does not depend on it in any way.**

**How much AI you use.** Using less is not more virtuous. Using more is not more advanced. What is assessed is judgement.

**Polish.** You are trained designers and you will be tempted to win on craft. Craft is necessary for B1 and B3 and irrelevant everywhere else. A beautiful object with no claim attached is not research; a modest object with a clear, evidenced, transferable claim is.

## Peer review instrument — session 12

**Part 1 — ranking.** Everyone presents; everyone ranks all other presenters on Presentation Format, Academic Contribution, and Personal Learning. Shared with Section 002.

**Part 2 — paired deep review.** You are given one classmate's draft and you review it **using the real review form their target venue gives its own reviewers** — the one they tracked down in A3 — writing your review in that venue's format.

One element is compulsory: **name the single weakest claim in the paper.** Not the weakest section — the weakest claim. Quote it, and say why it does not hold.

Reviewing teaches more about academic writing than any lecture. Take the reviewing half as seriously as the presenting half.
