## What this course asks of you

By session 14 — Wednesday 25 November — you will have made something, and you will have a **submission-ready research paper** about what making it revealed — aimed at a venue you chose and studied yourself.

Most of you are trained designers. Some of you have never written code, and few of you have published academically. Both of those are expected, and neither is the obstacle you think it is.

The obstacle is different. **A portfolio shows what you made. A paper argues what is now known that was not known before.** Closing that gap is what this course teaches.

---

## The AI method, plainly

You will use **Cursor**, loaded with the context documents in this folder, as a research partner throughout the term. This is not a shortcut and it is not optional.

It is also **not a licence to have your paper written for you.** The line runs like this:

**For code and making** — you direct, the agent implements. You are assessed on the decisions, the debugging, and being able to explain the result. You are not assessed on having typed it.

**For the paper** — you write. The claim, the interpretation, the argument and the prose are yours. The agent finds things, checks things, critiques things, and fixes your grammar. It does not write your argument.

The reason is not rule-following. A paper you defend in a viva, revise in response to reviewers, and build a career on requires that you know why every sentence is there. Prose written for you is a debt that comes due later.

There is one meta-level outcome of this course beyond the paper and the artifact: **learning to use these tools to do research well.** That skill will outlast any particular project.

## First session — 20 minutes

> **One thing first, before any of this.** Your very first reading note — the one due in session 2 — is written **by hand, with no AI tool of any kind.** Not because AI is discouraged here; it is required for most of what follows. Because you cannot supervise a tool at a task you have never performed yourself, and the reading note is the task the whole reading stream is built on. Do one properly, unassisted, and then the tools are open. The single permanent exception, all term: never ask an agent to write the critical thinking section.

1. **Install Cursor** and sign in. A subscription is required for the term.
2. **Create a project folder** for your work. Everything lives here.
3. **Copy this whole folder into it**, keeping the structure.
4. **Open Cursor** in that folder and start a chat.
5. **Load the agent file** — drag `research_agent.md` into the chat, or type `@research_agent.md`.
6. Ask it to set up your workspace:

```
@research_agent.md

Set up my workspace for ARTD8105. Create _research_log/ with the structure
in 03_Provenance_Logging_Spec.md, initialise git, make the first commit,
and confirm what you created. Then tell me what Step 1 asks of me.
```

7. **Set up git and GitHub** — `06_Git_and_GitHub_Setup.md`. Twenty minutes, once. Add the instructor as a read-only collaborator; this is the step people skip and it is invisible until it matters.
8. **Download the free readings** into a `readings/` folder — `07_Readings_and_Sources.md`. They become Cursor context.
9. **Create `process_journal/`** and write your first entry within twenty-four hours of session 1. The template is in `templates/`. The journal is graded, it cannot be reconstructed later, and in session 11 you will read the whole thing back looking for your finding.

**Then, at the start of every session from now on**, paste this:

```
@research_agent.md @03_Provenance_Logging_Spec.md

Start a logging session. Create today's session file in _research_log/sessions/
and log every substantive exchange per the spec, including disposition records
for the previous turn. I'm on workbook step S__.
```

The agent file does not persist between sessions, and neither does the logging instruction. Four short rituals carry the whole system: **start a session · `LOG CHECK` if it goes quiet · close the session · weekly summary.**

## The three things that go wrong

**Skipping the reading.** Step 1 asks you to read six papers before proposing anything. Everyone wants to skip it. In research, novelty is *defined* by the literature — if you propose before you read, you will discover in session 12 that your question was answered in 2019.

**Accepting the first plausible output.** The agent is fluent and confident and often wrong. Students who accept its first suggestion produce work that is competent, generic, and unpublishable. The habit that matters is rejecting things and knowing why. Your rejections are recorded and assessed.

**Leaving documentation to the end.** Process documentation is unrecoverable once you have moved on. The failed print, the intermediate state, the version that did not work — photograph it now or lose it. Same for the journal: memory smooths out exactly the stumbles that carry the knowledge.

## About the log

The agent keeps a record of your work as you go — what you asked, what it produced, what you did with it. **You do not compose the entries. You start the session, and the agent writes them while you work.**

It exists for two reasons. It is your evidence of authorship, which the rubric assesses. And it is the material you analyse in Step 8 to find what your project actually revealed — that analysis is where most students' findings come from.

**The log is submitted and graded at the end of term**, along with a one-page self-assessment of it. That self-assessment asks you to say honestly where logging lapsed and why. A record with a marked gap is more useful than one claiming unbroken coverage — write the honest version, it costs you nothing.

You review a weekly summary and correct anything wrong. That review is what makes the log yours rather than the machine's.

The log is a coursework artifact. If any of it is used in research about the course, that will be asked separately and after grades are final, and saying no carries no consequence of any kind.

## Two verification habits, from day one

**Every citation gets checked before it enters any document of yours.** Open the DOI. Confirm the title, authors, year and venue exist as stated.

This is not paranoia. **Two of the three "required readings" listed in this course's own accreditation document do not exist** — plausible titles, plausible publishers, no such books. That is what unverified citations look like when nobody checks them and they survive into an official document. A single fabricated citation in your paper caps that criterion at 1 regardless of how good the rest is.

**Every line of code you submit, you can explain.** You may not have typed it. You must be able to read it, say what it does, and change it. You will be asked, in person, without warning.

## What you should be able to say by 25 November

- Your contribution, in one sentence, without describing the artifact
- The log entry where your central finding first showed up as a surprise
- Three things you rejected from the agent, and why
- What any line of your code does
- What your target venue's standard is, and where you derived it from

If you can say those five things, the paper is a formality. If you cannot, no amount of writing will fix it.
