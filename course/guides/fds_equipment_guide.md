## Why this equipment matters for this course

This course is about **building systems that make design** — rule-based, generative, agentic
systems, in the lineage that runs from Sol LeWitt's wall-drawing instructions through Karl
Gerstner's *Designing Programmes* to the code you will be writing this term.

The temptation with that kind of work is to leave it on screen. The reason to walk over to Block 13
is that these machines are the **output devices** of a rule-based system, and they each impose
their own material logic on it:

- The **jacquard loom** is the historical ancestor of the stored program. One hook lifts one warp
  end; a design is literally a bitmap, and the image *is* the structure, not ink on top of it.
- The **embroidery machine**, the **vinyl cutter** and the **laser cutter** are plotters. They turn
  a vector system into a physical path, at speed, in editions.
- The **knitting machine** decides knit / tuck / miss for every needle in every row — a cellular
  automaton that produces cloth.
- The **3D printers** materialise generative geometry, and the dual-nozzle machine with soluble
  support lets you print forms that cannot be printed any other way.
- The **3D scanner** closes the loop: the physical world goes back into the system as a mesh.

And Block 15 is the **input** side. The EEG caps, the EDA sensors, the eye trackers and the
trajectory analyser let a system respond to a body. Read the honesty section in Part 2 before you
plan a project around them — what those instruments actually measure is narrower and noisier than
the marketing suggests, and for this course that gap is *more* interesting than the promise.

The single most useful reframing: **a machine's constraints are parameters of your algorithm.** An
embroidery machine breaks needles above a certain stitch density. A laser has a kerf width. A
knitting machine has one fixed gauge. If your generative system does not know those numbers, it
will produce beautiful output that cannot be made. Building the constraint into the rule set is the
work.

## How to read an entry

Every machine below has the same shape:

- **What it is** — plainly, including what it is *not*.
- **What it can and can't do** — the honest envelope.
- **Use it in this course** — concrete project shapes, tied to what we're doing this term.
- **Getting started** — the workflow, the file formats, skill level, and realistically how long
  before you have something in your hand.
- **Learn it** — three to five specific resources, with links.

**Confidence marks.** Specifications are marked **[V]** where they come from a manufacturer's own
documentation, and **[?]** where the model could not be confirmed from public sources and the
figures are typical-for-the-class rather than measured. Anything marked **[?]** should be checked
against the machine itself before you plan around it.

**Sources.** The equipment lists come from the FDS facilities page, which is authoritative here.
Model numbers come from the FDS equipment spreadsheet where a row matches. **Annex A** records
exactly where the two disagree — including one room that appears on the website with no spreadsheet
entry at all.

### 10 Needle Embroidery Machine

**Listed model: Brother PR680W** *(spreadsheet)*

#### What it is

A multi-needle flat embroidery machine — a **plotter that draws in thread**. A pantograph moves a
hooped, stabilised piece of cloth under a fixed sewing head, while a rotating needle-bar case
switches automatically between needles pre-loaded with different colours. It embroiders and nothing
else: no seams, no garment construction, no quilting.

**Note on the needle count.** The website lists this as a **10 Needle** machine, and the website is
the authority. The spreadsheet's model number, **PR680W**, is a **six**-needle machine in Brother's
catalogue **[V]**; Brother's ten-needle machine in the same family is the PR1055X. Either the
spreadsheet is out of date or the label is loose. **It matters practically** — six needles means
six colours before someone has to re-thread by hand, ten means ten. Count the needle bars on the
head when you get there; it takes five seconds and it determines how many colours your design can
have.

#### What it can and can't do

**Can:** embroider onto anything that can be held flat and taut in a hoop — T-shirts, denim,
canvas, felt, towelling, tote bags, thin leather. Caps and cylindrical items with the optional cap
frame. Metallic, variegated and specialty threads. Very fine line work, at a resolution set by
stitch length rather than pixels.

**Can't:** handle anything rigid or thicker than the presser-foot clearance. Work outside the hoop
— your design is hard-limited to the hoop dimensions. Sew a garment together. Print a photograph
(it can *stitch* a photograph, via density mapping, but that is a digitising skill, not a button).

**Specs for the PR680W [V]:** 6 needles · max embroidery area **200 × 300 mm** (8″ × 12″) · up to
**1,000 stitches/min** · 10.1″ touchscreen · crosshair laser positioning to ±0.5 mm · wireless LAN,
two USB ports · hoops from 1.5″×2″ up to 8″×12″ · 100–240 V.

#### Use it in this course

**Stitch as an algorithmic mark.** The stitch path is a continuous line with a minimum length, a
maximum density and a cost per colour change. That is an unusually rich constraint set for a
generative system — much richer than a pen plotter. A flow field, an L-system, a space-filling
curve or a differential-growth line all become physically different objects when the medium fights
back.

**A LeWitt instruction executed in thread.** *"A line from each corner of the hoop toward a
randomly chosen point, in three colours, never crossing."* The machine executes it identically
every time; the cloth does not. The variance between runs on different fabrics is the piece.

**Density as the subject.** Push the stitch density past what the fabric can carry and it puckers,
tears, breaks needles. There is a real threshold, and a system that finds it — that generates right
up to the edge of failure — is a good project. Just clear it with the admin first; needles are
cheap, machine downtime is not.

**Wearable output for the HCI strand.** If your project ends in something a person wears, this is
how the visual system gets onto the garment. Pairs naturally with the vinyl cutter's heat-transfer
workflow.

#### Getting started

**File formats: `.pes`, `.phc`, `.dst` only.** **[V]** Nothing else loads. `.jef`, `.exp`, `.vp3`
and `.hus` will be rejected — this is the single most common first-attempt failure.

**The critical `.dst` gotcha:** DST files carry **no colour information**. The machine substitutes a
default palette and you reassign every colour by hand at the console. Export `.pes` where you can.

**Workflow:**

1. **Digitise.** Turn artwork into stitch data. Free and open-source: **Ink/Stitch**, an Inkscape
   extension that exports both PES and DST — and, importantly for this course, it is scriptable, so
   a generative system can emit SVG that Ink/Stitch converts. Commercial: Brother PE-DESIGN 11,
   Wilcom Hatch.
2. **Transfer** by USB stick, or wirelessly from PE-DESIGN.
3. **Assign** each colour block to a needle bar at the console.
4. **Hoop** the fabric with the right stabiliser — cut-away for knits, tear-away for wovens,
   water-soluble for lace and for anything where the backing must vanish. Stabiliser choice is the
   actual craft skill here and it is what separates a clean result from a puckered one.
5. **Position** with the laser crosshair, trace the outline to check it fits, run.

**Skill level:** low to operate, **high to digitise**. A built-in design on a flat T-shirt:
**30–60 minutes** supervised. Your own generative artwork stitching out cleanly: **a day or more**,
almost all of it in digitising.

**Consumables:** HAx130EBBR needles (size 11/75 typical) **[?]**, 40 wt polyester or rayon
embroidery thread, prewound bobbins, stabiliser, temporary spray adhesive, machine oil.

#### Learn it

- **Brother PR680W Embroidery Design Guide (PDF)** — the digitising-side manual —
  <https://download.brother.com/welcome/doch102303/884t23_edg01endefrnlitesptru.pdf>
- **Ink/Stitch** — free, open-source, Inkscape-based, exports PES and DST — <https://inkstitch.org>

### Computerized Knitting Machine

**Listed model: "GS-S252"** *(spreadsheet)*

#### What it is

A **computerised V-bed flat knitting machine**. Two opposed needle beds meet in an inverted V; a
carriage traverses across them, and cam systems drive each selected needle to **knit, tuck or
miss**. Because selection is electronic and per-needle, every needle in every row is individually
programmable — which is what lets the machine produce shaped panels, jacquard, intarsia and fully
fashioned 3D forms without anyone touching it mid-run.

**On the model number.** No machine called exactly "GS-S252" appears in any manufacturer catalogue.
The string maps closely onto **Changshu Guosheng**'s computerised flat-knitting line, whose models
all use a `GS-` prefix and a `252` suffix meaning **52-inch knitting width** — the GS-SE252,
GS-SC252 and GS-SEC252 **[V]**. Read the nameplate to find out which. **[?]**

#### What it can and can't do

**The one number that governs everything is gauge** — needles per inch. 3G is chunky and
hand-knit-looking; 7G is standard sweater weight; 12–14G is fine jersey; 16G is very fine.
**A machine is one gauge and it cannot be changed.** Find out what the UM machine is before you buy
a single cone of yarn, because gauge dictates every yarn choice available to you.

**Accepts:** cone-wound, waxed knitting yarns — wool, acrylic, cotton, cashmere, nylon, polyester
and blends — at a count matched to the gauge.

**Will not handle:** yarn too thick for the gauge (it breaks needles), too thin (dropped stitches),
hairy, slubby or handspun yarn that jams the feeders, metallic or monofilament yarn that abrades
the latches, or anything not properly cone-wound.

**Typical specs for the GS-SE252 / GS-SC252 [V]:** 52″ knitting width · gauges 5G–16G depending on
build · double system, single carriage · carriage speed to **1.4 m/s** · 8 or 16 yarn carriers on
four guide rails · stepper-controlled stitch density, 128 sections · 256 MB memory · **USB** input ·
220 V single phase with power-loss memory.

#### Use it in this course

**Knit / tuck / miss is a three-state cellular automaton.** Every needle, every row, one of three
states, with neighbourhood effects that are physical rather than notional — a tuck stitch pulls on
its neighbours, a float carries across a run of misses. Rule 30 knitted at 7 gauge is not a
metaphor for computation, it *is* the computation, and the fabric distorts in ways the rule alone
does not predict. That gap is the interesting part.

**Fully fashioned generative form.** The machine can shape a panel by adding and removing needles
at the edges. A growth algorithm that outputs a shaping schedule rather than an outline produces a
garment piece that was grown, not cut.

**Structure as image.** Jacquard and intarsia let you place colour by needle. But unlike printing,
the colour change *is* a structural change — floats on the back, different fabric weight, different
drape. A generative image made this way behaves differently depending on what it depicts.

**A realistic warning.** This is the hardest machine in the building to self-teach, and the most
likely to consume a whole project's time in setup. If you want knitted output within one term,
scope it small: a swatch series that tests one rule at a range of parameter values is a genuinely
good project. A garment is not.

#### Getting started

**Software.** Chinese computerised flat knitters overwhelmingly run **Zhejiang Hengqiang (HQCNC)**
control systems with their **HQ-PDS pattern design software** (Windows) **[?]**. You draw the
stitch-symbol chart, or import a bitmap for jacquard, set carriers / racking / take-down / density
per row, compile to machine data, and take it over on a **USB stick [V]**.

**The file extension the Guosheng–Hengqiang chain uses could not be confirmed from public sources.**
Find it out at the machine before you write anything that generates pattern files. **[?]**

**Skill level:** high. A plain swatch from an existing stock program, with a trained operator
present: **1–3 hours**. Your own designed, shaped panel: **days to weeks**.

#### Learn it

- **Changshu Guosheng GS-SC252 product page** — <https://www.flatknitting-machine.com/shop/gs-sc252>

  <https://www.hqcnc.com/product/354.html>

### Electronic Jacquard Device

**Listed model: "SGA598"** *(spreadsheet)*

#### What it is

A **bench-scale rapier sample loom with an electronic jacquard head**. It weaves fabric samples at
production quality but at swatch scale, so a designer can test a structure before committing a
production loom to it.

"SGA598" is a Chinese textile-machinery **type designation** rather than one company's model — at
least two manufacturers build machines under it, including **Jiangyin Tongyuan**, whose catalogue
includes both semi-automatic and fully-automatic jacquard variants **[V]**. Which one is in DZ
13-102, and with how many hooks, has to be read off the machine.

#### What it can and can't do

The jacquard head lifts **each warp end individually** — one hook, one end. There are no heald
frames and therefore no repeat limit across the width. **Any** weave structure, or any pictorial
image, can be woven pick by pick.

**Hook count is your horizontal resolution.** Tongyuan's semi-automatic jacquard variant offers
**1,408 or 2,688 hooks** across a **50 cm** width; the fully-automatic goes to **6,144** across
20–70 cm, with 8-colour electronic weft selection and pattern input by **USB or LAN** **[V]**. At
1,408 hooks over 50 cm that is about **28 ends per centimetre** — your design is a bitmap whose
pixel width equals the hook count. Vertical resolution is picks per centimetre, set by take-up.

Speed is **30–45 picks per minute** **[V]**. That is slow. A 500-pick sample is roughly a
quarter-hour of weaving — and several days of warp preparation before it.

**Accepts** cotton, wool, silk, linen, man-made fibres and blends **[V]**, plus monofilament and
technical yarns in research use. **Will not** cope with yarn too coarse for the heddle eyes and
reed dents, or with low-twist hairy warp yarn that sheds and jams the shed — which is exactly why
sizing machines exist in this product family.

#### Use it in this course

**This is the most historically loaded machine in the building, and the most on-theme.** The
Jacquard loom's punched cards are the direct ancestor of the stored program — Babbage took the idea
from it, Ada Lovelace wrote that the Analytical Engine "weaves algebraic patterns just as the
Jacquard loom weaves flowers and leaves." Working on it is not an analogy for computation; it is
where computation came from.

**The image is the structure.** Nothing is printed. What you see is which ends are up and which are
down — binary, at 28 per centimetre. A generative bitmap woven here has a different ontological
status from the same bitmap printed on the plotter next door, and that difference is worth a paper.

**Weave structure as a rule system.** Assign different binary structures — satin, twill, sateen —
to different regions of a generated image and the cloth acquires different hand, sheen and drape
per region. Float length is a hard constraint: too long and the fabric snags and destabilises. Your
algorithm has to know that.

**Scope honestly.** Warp preparation is roughly 90% of the work and 90% of the failures. One
carefully considered sample is a complete project. A series of samples exploring one parameter is
an ambitious one.

#### Getting started

1. Build the design as a **bitmap where one pixel = one warp end × one pick**, at a width matching
   the hook count.
2. Assign **weave structures** to colour areas in jacquard CAD. The machine ships with vendor
   software; the free open-source alternative is **Digibunai**, and the professional references are
   ArahWeave, NedGraphics and PointCarré.
3. Generate the machine pattern file.
4. Transfer by **USB or LAN** on the fully-automatic variant **[V]**, or through the supplied
   software on the semi-automatic.
5. Warp, size if needed, draw in, weave.

The loom is the *last* step in a four-machine chain: sample warper → single-yarn sizing → sample
drawing-in machine → loom.

**Skill level:** high, specialist. First sample from a standing start, supervised: **1–3 days**.

#### Learn it

- **Jiangyin Tongyuan SGA598 semi-automatic sample loom (English)** — <http://www.tongyuan-fj.com/viewprut_en.asp?id=21>
- **Tongyuan SGA598 semi-automatic jacquard variant (Chinese)** — <http://www.tongyuan-fj.com/viewprut.asp?id=20>
- **Tongyuan SGA598 fully-automatic jacquard variant (Chinese)** — <http://www.tongyuan-fj.com/viewprut.asp?id=24>
- **Digibunai** — free open-source jacquard and dobby weaving CAD — <https://digibunai.dic.gov.in/index.php/about>

### Desktop Engraver

**Listed model: Roland CAMM-1 GS-24** *(spreadsheet)*

#### What it is

Named "Desktop Engraver" on the FDS list. The spreadsheet's model, the **Roland CAMM-1 GS-24**, is
a **desktop cutting plotter — a vinyl cutter**. It drags a small swivel-mounted carbide blade
through thin sheet media while a grit-roller feed shuttles the material back and forth. **[V]**

**If the machine in the room is the GS-24, it has no spindle, no rotary tool and no laser, and it
cannot engrave anything.** Roland's engravers are a different line (CAMM-3 / EGX); the naming
similarity is the likely source of the label. Check before you plan an engraving project — and if
you need engraving, the laser cutter next door in 13-103 does it.

#### What it can and can't do

**Can:** cut self-adhesive vinyl, **heat-transfer vinyl and flock** (for textiles — directly useful
here), paint mask, sandblast resist, reflective vinyl, paper and light card, window frosting, thin
magnetic sheet. It does **print-and-cut** via an optical registration system that reads crop marks
printed on an ordinary desktop printer, plus perforation cutting, overlap cutting up to 10× for
thicker media, and cut-by-line-colour.

**Can't:** touch wood, acrylic, metal, glass or anything rigid. Media narrower than 50 mm.
Practically it tops out around 0.4 mm material thickness **[?]** — Roland publishes no thickness
figure.

**Specs [V]:** media width **50–700 mm** · max cutting area **584 mm wide × 25 m long** · speed to
**500 mm/s** · blade force **30–350 gf** · mechanical resolution 0.0125 mm/step · USB 2.0 ·
860 × 319 × 235 mm, 13.5 kg.

#### Use it in this course

**This is the best first machine in the building for turning an algorithm into an object**, and it
is the one to start with if you have never made anything physical from code. Low risk, low
consumable cost, fast iteration, and the output is a vector path — exactly what a generative system
already produces.

**A knife plotter is a pen plotter that removes material.** Everything from the plotter-art
tradition — Vera Molnár, Manfred Mohr, the whole 1960s computer-graphics lineage this course sits
in — transfers directly, but the output is a physical positive and negative rather than a drawn
line. The weeding step, where you pull away everything that isn't the design, makes figure/ground a
manual decision.

**Stencils and masks as a production system.** Cut a mask, spray through it, cut the next.
A generative system that outputs a *series* of masks gives you a print run where each pull is
algorithmically different — cheaper and faster than screen printing for short editions.

**Wearables.** Heat-transfer vinyl onto garments, paired with the heat press. If your project ends
on a body, this is the quickest route.

**Layered depth.** Multi-layer vinyl builds up physical thickness. A generative system that
separates its output into cut layers produces a low-relief object, not a print.

#### Getting started

**Workflow:** design vector artwork in **Illustrator, CorelDRAW, Inkscape** or Roland's own
**CutStudio** → send through the CutStudio driver or the Illustrator / CorelDRAW plug-in over USB →
set blade force, offset and speed → **run a cutting test** → cut → weed → apply transfer tape.

**A nuance that trips people up:** you generally do *not* hand this machine a `.plt` file. You
print through the CutStudio driver, and HP-GL/`.plt` is what the driver emits. Third-party packages
(SignCut, FlexiSIGN) can send `.plt` directly if you prefer that route. CutStudio itself imports
**BMP, JPG, STX, AI and EPS** **[V]** and can auto-trace bitmaps.

**The number-one beginner error is blade protrusion.** The blade tip should extend from the holder
only just past the material thickness — too far and it cuts through the liner and the protection
strip, too little and it under-cuts. Second most common: **offset** not matching the blade type
(0.25 mm for the general-purpose ZEC-U5025, 0.175 mm for the fine-detail ZEC-U3017, 0.5 mm for
cardboard), which rounds or overshoots every corner. Third: pinch rollers not sitting over the grit
rollers, so the media tracks off and skews — invisible until you're a metre into a long cut.

**Skill level:** low. First shape cut, weeded and applied: **15–45 minutes**. Print-and-cut
workflows: a few hours to get reliable.

#### Learn it

### High-speed Resin 3D Printer + Wash & Cure Station

**Listed brand: Formlabs** *(spreadsheet)*

#### What it is

A **stereolithography (SLA) resin printer** with its two post-processing partners: a **Form Wash**
(agitated isopropyl alcohol bath) and a **Form Cure** (405 nm UV oven). All three are one workflow;
a resin print is not finished when the printer stops.

Formlabs' current professional desktop machine is the **Form 4** **[V]**. If the machine here is a
Form 3 or 3+, it is the previous, laser-based generation — slower, same workflow.

#### What it can and can't do

**Form 4 specs [V]:** Low Force Display masked-SLA · build volume **200 × 125 × 210 mm** · **50 µm
XY pixel** · 100 µm standard layers, finer available · most prints under two hours · XY tolerance
±0.15% · around 23 resin families including tough, high-temperature, flame-retardant, elastomeric,
**Silicone 40A**, ceramic, castable and biocompatible · **Open Material Mode** accepts third-party
405 nm photopolymers.

**Form Cure V2 [V]:** 405 nm, 48 multi-directional LEDs, up to 100 °C, 60 seconds for
general-purpose resins, 3–15 minutes for engineering and biocompatible ones.

**Choose resin over FDM when** you need fine detail, smooth surfaces, thin walls, or transparency.
**Choose FDM when** you need size, toughness, speed, low cost, or you're iterating.

#### Use it in this course

**Detail that FDM cannot reach.** Anything where the generative geometry has features under a
millimetre — reaction-diffusion surfaces, dense lattices, fine filigree, small mechanisms.

**Scanned organic geometry.** The natural partner to the 3D scanner across the room: scan
something, transform it algorithmically, print it back at a resolution that keeps the detail the
scanner captured.

**Castable output.** The castable resin family burns out cleanly for lost-wax metal casting. A
generative form → resin → metal is a complete pipeline that ends in something with real material
presence.

#### Getting started

CAD or mesh → STL/OBJ/3MF → **PreForm** (Formlabs' free slicer; it auto-orients, generates supports
and runs a live printability check) → print → drain → **Form Wash** → dry → remove supports →
**Form Cure** → sand and finish.

**The most common student error is under-curing** — a part that's still tacky is also mechanically
weak, and it looks finished. The second is over-washing, which warps thin walls: set the timer,
don't leave parts soaking. Expect roughly 70 prints per IPA change; check saturation with the
supplied hydrometer.

**Skill level:** intermediate, mostly because of the post-processing discipline. First good part:
**half a day** including cure time.

#### Safety — read this one

This is the highest-hazard item in the room after the laser. **Uncured resin is a skin sensitiser**
— nitrile gloves every time, eye protection, and never wash resin off skin with IPA (it drives the
resin *into* the skin; use soap and water). **IPA is flammable**: closed containers, no open
flames, and it should not live next to the laser cutter. Uncured resin, contaminated IPA, used
gloves and paper towels are **chemical waste** — never down the drain, never in general refuse.
Cure waste resin fully before disposal. Each resin family has its own SDS; read the one you're
using.

### FDM 3D Printer H2D · H2S · P2S

**Listed models: Bambu Lab H2D (×3), H2S (×1), P2S (×2)** *(spreadsheet)*

Three machines from the same manufacturer, and the FDS list names them separately because they are
genuinely different tools. Common description first, then how to choose.

#### What they are

Enclosed **CoreXY fused-deposition printers** — a heated nozzle extrudes molten thermoplastic
filament layer by layer. All three are fast, well-instrumented and driven by the same slicer, and
all three support the **AMS** multi-material system for multi-colour and multi-material printing.

#### How to choose

| | **P2S** | **H2S** | **H2D** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Build volume | 256 × 256 × 256 mm | 340 × 320 × 340 mm | 325 × 320 × 325 mm (300 wide in dual mode) |
| Chamber | Enclosed, **passive** | **Actively heated to 65 °C** | **Actively heated to 65 °C** |
| Max nozzle / bed | 300 °C / 110 °C | 350 °C / 120 °C | 350 °C / 120 °C |
| Nozzles | 1 | 1 | **2, independent** |
| Top speed | 600 mm/s | 1000 mm/s | 1000 mm/s |
| Engineering materials (PC-CF, PPS, PPA) | No | Yes | Yes |
| Laser / cutter / pen modules | No | Optional **10 W** laser, blade, pen | Optional **10 W or 40 W** laser, blade, pen |
| Units at FDS | **2** | **1** | **3** |

**[V]** for all specs above.

**Pick the P2S** for coursework, iteration and anything in PLA, PETG or ABS. Two units and the
lowest queue pressure — this is your default.

**Pick the H2S** when the part is bigger than 256 mm, or when it needs an engineering polymer. The
heated chamber is the entire point: it is what stops ABS, ASA, PA and PC from warping and
delaminating.

**Pick the H2D** for one of two specific reasons. First, **two materials printing simultaneously**
— above all a dedicated **soluble-support** nozzle (PVA or BVOH), which lets you print internal
geometry, captive moving parts and dense lattices that simply cannot be printed with break-away
supports. Second, the **40 W laser** — the H2S takes only the 10 W module, so the H2D is the
only machine here that cuts roughly 15 mm of basswood rather than roughly 5 mm.

**Materials, all three [V]:** PLA, PETG, TPU, PVA, BVOH, ABS, ASA, PC, PA, PET, plus carbon- and
glass-fibre variants; the H-series adds PPS and PPA. None of them will do PEEK or ULTEM. **TPU and
other flexibles cannot go through the AMS** — external spool holder only.

#### Use it in this course

**The obvious use — materialising generative geometry — is fine, but the interesting uses are
these:**

**Soluble support as a geometric unlock (H2D).** Most "impossible" generative forms are only
impossible because supports can't be removed from inside them. With a second nozzle laying down
dissolvable material, interlocking, nested and captive geometries print in one piece. If your system
generates lattices, gyroids, or forms with enclosed voids, this is the machine.

**Print farm as a parameter sweep.** Six FDM machines across three models means you can run a
*series*. Generate twenty variants, print them, and lay them out on a table — the comparison is the
work, and it is a genuinely different mode of thinking from rendering twenty images.

**Failure as material.** Deliberately violating the slicer's assumptions — overhangs past 45°,
walls thinner than the nozzle, deliberate under-extrusion — produces a distinctive vocabulary of
sag, string and gap. A system that generates *toward* those failure modes rather than away from
them is a good project, and one you should clear with Prof. Liu first.

**The H2D as a multi-tool.** If the laser and blade modules are fitted, a single generative output
can be cut, drawn and printed on the same bed with registered coordinates. That is a rare capability
and worth investigating.

#### Getting started

Mesh → STL or 3MF → **Bambu Studio** (free, Windows and macOS, derived from PrusaSlicer) → slice →
send over LAN or Wi-Fi, or export G-code to USB. **OrcaSlicer** is the community alternative but its
H2D dual-nozzle support has lagged behind Bambu Studio.

**Skill level:** low. **First successful print within about an hour.** This is the most
beginner-friendly equipment in the building.

**Gotchas:** wet filament (PA, PVA, PETG absorb moisture fast in Macau) causes stringing and weak
layers — use the AMS dryer. Chamber heat softens PLA, so run PLA on the H-series with the door open
or the heater off. Skin oils on the PEI plate cause adhesion failures; wash it with dish soap, not
just IPA. Carbon-fibre filament destroys brass nozzles — hardened steel only. On the H2D, dual-nozzle
purge waste is significant; plan a prime tower or flush into the object.

- **Bambu Lab Wiki — P2S** — <https://wiki.bambulab.com/en/p2s>

### Silicone Printer

**Listed model: sanDraw S400** *(spreadsheet)*

#### What it is

A **two-part platinum-cure silicone additive manufacturing machine**. Two silicone components are
metered, mixed and extruded through a fine nozzle; the formulation stays liquid but holds its shape
while stacking, and the finished part is **oven-cured** afterwards. The material is **100% pure
silicone** — not a "silicone-like" photopolymer resin, which is what most machines advertising
soft output actually print. **[V]**

The manufacturer is **San Draw Inc.**, based in Taichung, Taiwan. **[V]**

#### What it can and can't do

**Specs [V]:** layer thickness **0.1–0.2 mm** · nozzle **0.3–0.4 mm** · materials SIL18 through
SIL70, i.e. **Shore A 18 to 70** — from very soft to firm rubber · support material is either
silicone or **water-soluble SP65** · **oven heat cure** after printing · software **FAMufacture** or
**Cura**, input **STL** · USB or SD card, Windows. It will also print hydrogels, ceramic gels and
food gels.

**Build volume: 150 × 200 × 100 mm** on San Draw's own English spec table — the *smallest* of their
three machines, despite the S400 being the flagship. The "large-size" description refers to its
motorised screw feed and ball-screw motion, not to its build envelope. San Draw's English and
Chinese tables disagree about which volume belongs to which model, so confirm before you design to
it. **[?]**

**Surface finish is visibly layered.** This is not a moulding process; do not expect the finish of
a cast part.

#### Use it in this course

**Soft robotics and pneumatic actuators.** Printed silicone with internal channels inflates and
bends. This is the entry point to a whole body of work — soft grippers, crawling forms, inflatable
surfaces — and it connects directly to the artificial-life strand of the course. A generative system
that designs channel networks and then tests them physically is a genuinely strong project.

**Tactile interfaces for the HCI strand.** Shore A 18 to 70 is a wide expressive range. A
generative system that varies durometer across a surface produces something that feels different in
different places — an interface you read with your fingers.

**Wearables that touch skin.** Silicone is the right material for anything worn against the body,
which makes it the natural output for projects that also use the Block 15 biosignal sensors —
printed electrode housings, sensor mounts, straps.

**Casting masters and moulds.** Print the mould, not the part.

#### Getting started

CAD → STL → FAMufacture or Cura → USB/SD → print → remove from platform → **oven post-cure** →
dissolve SP65 supports in water → deflash and trim.

**The discipline that matters:** two-part silicone has a pot life, and mixed material left sitting
in the static mixing nozzle will cure inside it and destroy it. Purge and flush every time.
Overhangs need support — unlike FDM, uncured silicone sags.

**Platinum-cure silicone is inhibited by sulphur, latex, tin-cure silicone and some tapes.** If any
of those contaminate the work, the part will not cure and you will not know why. Keep the area
clean and keep latex gloves away from it — nitrile only.

**Skill level:** intermediate to advanced. **A full day** with a trained operator for a first good
part; about a week to be reliable.

### 3D Scanner

**Listed model: Shining 3D FreeScan Combo** *(spreadsheet)*

#### What it is

A **metrology-grade handheld 3D scanner** that combines a **blue laser** mode with an **infrared
VCSEL structured-light** mode — hence "Combo". It captures **geometry only: no colour, no
texture**. **[V]**

#### What it can and can't do

**Specs [V]:** accuracy to **0.02 mm** · volumetric accuracy 0.020 + 0.033 mm/m · point distance
0.05–3 mm (laser), 0.2–3 mm (infrared) · **1.86 million points/s** laser, 2.25 million infrared ·
**26 parallel laser lines** for speed, 7 for detail, **1 single line** for deep pockets and holes ·
field of view to 520 × 510 mm · working distance 300 mm · **Class II blue laser, eye-safe** ·
620 g · certified to VDI/VDE 2634 Part 3 and ISO 10360.

**Can scan** dark, matte-black and shiny surfaces — this is the main reason to own a blue-laser
scanner rather than a white-light one, which struggles badly with both. Deep holes via single-line
mode. Feature-rich parts without markers in infrared mode.

**Cannot scan** hair, fur, fabric fuzz, transparent glass, clear acrylic, mirror chrome at grazing
angles, or anything that moves. **No colour or texture at all** — if your project needs a textured
mesh, this is the wrong tool and you should photogrammetry it instead.

**It needs a serious PC**: minimum i7-8700F, 16 GB, **RTX 3060 or better**; recommended i7-10700+,
32 GB, 2 TB SSD **[V]**. The **i9 workstation in DZ 15-102** meets that comfortably; the other machine has 16 GB,
which is the bare minimum, so mesh on the i9.

**Exports** STL, OBJ, PLY, ASC, 3MF. Windows only.

#### Use it in this course

**Close the loop.** This is the machine that lets the physical world back into your system. Scan an
object, an offcut, a hand, a piece of the campus → mesh → run it through a generative
transformation → print it back. A pipeline that goes world → data → rule → world is a much stronger
project shape than one that starts in CAD.

**Found geometry as seed.** A scanned mesh is messy, asymmetric and full of noise in a way that
nothing generated in Rhino ever is. Feeding real geometry into a system that expects clean input is
productive: the noise becomes part of the output.

**Iteration on physical work.** Make something by hand in the wood workshop, scan it, let the system
propose variants, print them, hand-modify again, rescan. That back-and-forth between hand and
algorithm is exactly the kind of AI co-creation loop this course is about, only with geometry rather
than text.

**Documentation and archiving.** A scan is a record. If your work is physical and ephemeral, this
is how it survives.

#### Getting started

Calibrate on the supplied board → new project → choose mode and resolution → scan from multiple
angles → align by markers, features, or manually → mesh → export. Bundled **FreeScan** software, no
subscription. Downstream: Geomagic Design X, PolyWorks, EXModel.

**Gotchas:** skipping calibration is the number-one cause of bad data. Marker spacing that is too
regular confuses alignment. Direct sunlight and flickering fluorescents add noise. Large featureless
flat or cylindrical surfaces need markers — the scanner has nothing to align on otherwise. Meshing a
20-million-point cloud on an underpowered laptop takes longer than the scan did.

**Skill level:** intermediate. **First usable mesh in 2–3 hours**; competence in about a week.

### Screen Printing Machine

**Listed brand: xTool** *(spreadsheet)*

#### What it is

The **xTool Screen Printer** — and it is not a standalone machine. It is two things: **pre-coated,
emulsion-free screens whose stencil is burned directly by an xTool laser**, and a **manual
screen-printing press** to pull the prints. It requires an xTool laser, sold separately. **[V]**

That is worth understanding because it eliminates the traditional barrier to screen printing
entirely: **no darkroom, no light-sensitive emulsion, no UV exposure unit, no washout booth.** You
go from vector file to burned stencil in minutes.

**Not to be confused with the xTool Apparel Printer**, which is a direct-to-film (DTF) machine —
different workflow, different consumables, no screens. Confirm which one is in the room.

**Specs [V]:** pre-coated screens rated to about **1,500 impressions** (vs ~200 for traditional
emulsion) · screen sizes 292 × 406 mm or 229 × 305 mm depending on laser · **mesh 100 and 200
count** · press with 3-axis micro-registration, 60° hover frame, Z-axis auto-press for objects up to
**60 mm thick**, magnetic squeegee · inks: water-based, plastisol, acrylic, puff, photochromic,
thermochromic, glow-in-the-dark · substrates: paper, fabric, silk, wood, acrylic, metal, glass,
ceramic.

Third-party inks are widely reported to work, but xTool does not state compatibility — treat that as
unverified and test on scrap. **[?]**

#### Use it in this course

**Editions.** This is the machine for making *many* of something. A generative system that produces
one image produces an image; a system that produces an edition of forty, each pull slightly
different in registration and ink coverage, produces a body of work. The hand-pulled variance sits
in productive tension with the algorithmic determinism upstream.

**Halftone as an algorithmic decision.** xTool's software has one-click colour separation, automatic
CMYK separation and halftone filters — but you do not have to use them. Writing your own halftone,
dither or error-diffusion algorithm and burning *that* into the screen puts a decision that is
usually invisible and automatic into the centre of the work. This is a strong, well-scoped project.

**Multi-colour registration as a constraint system.** Each colour needs its own screen and its own
frame, and registration drift is physical. A system designed to be robust to misregistration — or
one that exploits it — is more interesting than one that fights it.

**Print on anything.** Because the press takes objects up to 60 mm thick, you can print onto things
you made in the other workshops: laser-cut panels, 3D-printed surfaces, wood.

#### Getting started

1. Design in **xTool Creative Space (XCS)**.
2. Mount the pre-coated screen, remove the cardboard backing, tension until the mesh sounds like a
   drum.
3. Install the screen bracket **on the right side** of the laser, place the frame on the locating
   pins.
4. **Mirror the design horizontally**, position it in the engravable area, burn. One to thirty
   minutes depending on artwork. **The burn is permanent** — a mistake costs a screen.
5. Move the frame to the press, set platen position and screen height for the garment thickness.
6. Flood, lower, pull the squeegee at about 45°, lift.
7. Air-dry per the ink, then **heat-set**.
8. Wash the screen with water.

This is the one piece of safety knowledge that has to come before the machine, not after.

| Material | Why |
|---|---|
| **PVC, vinyl, any chlorinated plastic** | Releases **chlorine gas** — acutely toxic and it corrodes the machine and optics |
| **Polycarbonate / Lexan** | Absorbs 10.6 µm; flames, chars, yellows |

| **Self-adhesive materials, glued laminates, flame-retardant items** | Adhesives and retardants emit chlorine and bromine |
| **Coated or unknown metals** | Beam reflection damages the optics, and a CO₂ laser cannot cut them anyway |

---

### First: what these instruments actually measure

This matters more than any specification below, and getting it wrong is the most common way a
biosignal project fails.

**What you can measure robustly:**

- **Sympathetic arousal**, via electrodermal activity. This is the cleanest signal in the lab —
  sweat glands are innervated purely by the sympathetic nervous system, with no parasympathetic
  confound.
- **Where someone looked**, and in what order, and for how long. Genuinely reliable.
- **Spectral power in EEG frequency bands** — posterior alpha (8–13 Hz) rises reliably when the eyes
  close and relates to visual disengagement.
- **Event-related potentials** — P300 for attention and oddball detection, N170 for faces, N400 for
  semantic incongruity — **but only with tens to hundreds of clean repetitions per condition**. A
  study that shows twelve stimuli once each will not produce an ERP.
- **SSVEP** — a flickering stimulus at frequency *f* produces a measurable response at *f*. The most
  reliable readout EEG offers.
- **Hesitation during a choice**, via movement trajectory. A real and under-used method.

**What you cannot measure, whatever the vendor demonstration showed you:**

- **Emotion.** Physiology gives you arousal with reasonable fidelity and **valence essentially not at
  all**. Fear and excitement look the same. This is not a limitation of these particular
  instruments; it is a property of the signals.
- **Thoughts, words, images or intentions.** Nothing in this lab reads minds.
- **Single-trial, per-person answers** to "how did they feel about this design". Everything here is
  an aggregate measure that needs a within-participant baseline and many trials.

**And the thing nobody tells you first: the dominant practical problem is artefact, not signal.**
A blink is roughly a hundred times the amplitude of the EEG you are trying to record — scalp EEG is
10–100 µV, a blink is hundreds of µV to millivolts. Jaw clench, swallowing, neck tension, cable sway
and speech all contaminate heavily. Frontal and temporal EEG channels pick up facial muscle activity
directly, which is why so many published "emotion classifiers" are, on inspection, detecting that
the participant smiled.

**Why this is good news for this course.** A design faculty's instinct is to treat the gap between
what the instrument promises and what it delivers as a problem to hide. It is a better subject than
most projects will find on their own. A system driven by a signal that is honestly unreliable — and
that says so — is more interesting than one that pretends to read a mind. Make the noise visible.
Put the artefact in the work.

### Second: saline versus gel

FDS has three of each. They are the same instrument with different electrode chemistry, and the
choice determines the shape of your entire study.

| | **Saline (sponge)** | **Gel** |
|---|---|---|
| Target impedance | 60–100 kΩ | **5–10 kΩ** |
| Setup time, 32 channels | **under 5 minutes** | **about 25 minutes** |
| Usable recording | **60–90 minutes** before sponges dry | **up to 12 hours** |
| Participant afterwards | hair slightly damp | **must wash their hair** |
| Comfort | rated most comfortable | least |
| Noise immunity | lower | **higher** |

**[V]**, from Brain Products' published electrode-technology comparison.

**What the impedance numbers mean in practice.** Higher electrode impedance is not automatically
bad signal — it is worse *noise immunity*. A 60–100 kΩ saline electrode is a longer antenna for
50 Hz mains hum and for movement-induced potentials. In a shielded room with a still, seated
participant you may never notice. In a design studio with laptops, LED lighting and phone chargers,
and a participant who moves, you will.

**Choose saline for:** teaching, workshops, exploratory pilots, high-throughput studies, and
anything where participants have to go straight back to class looking normal.

**Choose gel for:** ERP studies, publication-grade work, any claim about topography or
lateralisation, long protocols, and anything involving movement — saline's contact is the first
thing to fail when a participant moves.

**The invisible failure that ruins saline data: bridging.** Over-wetting runs saline across the
scalp and electrically connects adjacent electrodes. Two channels silently become one, and every
spatial claim you make afterwards is false. Soak the sponges **in a cup, off the head**, squeeze out
the excess, and never hydrate while the participant is wearing the cap. A few drops of glycerine in
the felt slows evaporation. Gel bridges the same way if you over-inject.

**What a session actually costs in time:** roughly **one hour per participant with saline**, and
**1.5 to 2 hours with gel** including cleanup and the participant washing their hair. If your plan
says "twenty participants in an afternoon", the plan is wrong.

### Third: the ethics gate

**Every instrument in this room requires ethics approval before you record a single participant —
including pilots, class projects and "I just tested it on my flatmate".** Recording physiological
signals from a person is human-subjects research, and there is no informal tier.

UM runs a **Research Ethics Review System (RERS)** through RSKTO, with panels including Social
Science & Humanities and Biomedical Science & Engineering. Design human-factors work with biosignals
sits ambiguously between the two — **ask RSKTO which panel applies rather than guessing**, because
submitting to the wrong one costs weeks.

- Portal: <https://forms.researchethics.um.edu.mo> (UMPASS login)
- Guidance and applicant guides: <https://rskto.um.edu.mo/um-research-ethics-review-system/>

**What your protocol has to address for this equipment specifically:**

1. **Informed consent** covering what is recorded, that these devices are **not diagnostic**, and
   the right to stop mid-session.
2. **Incidental findings.** State explicitly that no clinical interpretation will be made or
   communicated. You are not qualified to, and the device is not validated for it.
3. **Physical risk and hygiene.** Gel preparation involves mild skin abrasion, which breaks the skin
   barrier. You need a documented electrode disinfection procedure and a policy on scalp conditions.
   Caps are shared head-contact items.
4. **Data protection.** Biosignal data is personal data under Macau's **Personal Data Protection Act
   (Law 8/2005)**. Raw EEG and gaze data are increasingly regarded as potentially re-identifying, so
   "anonymised" is a claim you must be able to defend. Keep the participant-code-to-identity key
   separate from the data.
5. **VR** (the Focus Vision): simulator-sickness screening, photosensitivity warning, a stop rule and
   a supervised play space.
6. **Eye-tracking glasses in public space:** the scene camera records **bystanders who did not
   consent**. This needs an explicit plan — restricted areas, signage, face blurring, or destroying
   the scene video after coding.

**Plan for this to take time.** If a biosignal project is going into your final work, the ethics
application needs to be in early in the term, not after the pilot.

#### What it is

A **wrist-worn multi-sensor ambulatory physiological logger** — the research-grade equivalent of an
Empatica-style band. Per the vendor's page it records **PPG (optical pulse), heart rate and HRV,
SpO₂, EDA, skin temperature, tri-axial accelerometry**, plus ambient pressure, temperature and light.
Wired and wireless, online and offline logging, with a **physical event-marker button**. **[V]**

**Sampling rates, channel counts, battery life and resolution are not published.** Do not quote

#### What it can and can't do

**Be precise about what these signals are.** PPG is an *optical* measure of blood volume at the
skin — it is not ECG. You derive inter-beat intervals from PPG peaks and HRV from those intervals,
which means motion corrupting the optical signal propagates directly into your HRV numbers. **Wrist
PPG under movement is the weakest link in the device.** And **wrist EDA is substantially weaker and
noisier than finger or palm EDA**, where the sweat gland density is highest — the wrist is a
convenience compromise, not a quality one. Skin temperature here tracks peripheral vasoconstriction,
not core temperature.

**Realistically obtainable:** within-participant, baseline-corrected arousal trends over minutes;
movement and posture context; event-marked segments of a long design session.

**Not obtainable:** emotion category, valence, or any cross-person "engagement score" that means
anything.

#### Use it in this course

**Long-duration, in-the-wild recording is the thing this device does that nothing else here can.**
The EEG caps constrain a participant to a chair for ninety minutes. This one goes into a studio, a
workshop, a walk across campus, and runs for hours with a button the wearer presses when something
happens.

**Self-report versus physiology.** The event-marker button gives you the wearer's *own* account of
when something mattered; the sensors give you a physiological trace. Where they disagree is the
finding, and it is a much better research question than "can we detect emotion" — because the answer
to that one is no.

**Ambient sensors are underrated.** Light, temperature and pressure alongside physiology lets you
ask whether the environment, not the task, is driving the signal. In a design studio it very often
is.

**Skill level:** intermediate. The hardware is easy; interpreting HRV correctly is not.

#### Getting started

Export to CSV and analyse in **NeuroKit2** (Python), which has proper EDA decomposition and PPG/HRV
pipelines. Expect to spend more time on artefact rejection than on analysis — use the accelerometer
channel to throw out movement-contaminated epochs, which is what it is there for.

**Gotchas:** the band must be snug but not tight. EDA needs **5–10 minutes of acclimatisation**
after donning before values stabilise. Roughly 5–10% of people are EDA "non-responders" with nearly
flat traces — that is normal physiology, not a broken sensor. **Never compute HRV over 30-second
windows**; most metrics are unstable below about two minutes and frequency-domain metrics need five.

### Smart Interactive BCI (Brain-Computer Interface) Edge-computing Instrument

**Vendor: 北京恒挚 · Qty 1** *(spreadsheet)*

**Model unresolved [?].** Two plausible catalogue matches: **SmartBCI x24/x32/x64** (24, 32 or 64
channels, wireless, portable, supporting both dry electrodes and conductive paste), or
**DB-EEG-MP-ONE**, a genuinely miniature amplifier — 1 channel expandable to 8 by networking, 3.7 g,
BLE, five hours at 1 kHz **[V]**. Those are very different instruments; check which is in the room.

#### What it is

**"Edge computing" here means the classification runs on the device rather than a tethered PC** — it
outputs *decisions* ("target 3 selected") rather than only microvolts. That is a packaging claim, not
a capability claim. The underlying paradigms are the standard ones.

#### What a non-invasive BCI actually does

Three paradigms work, in descending order of usefulness:

- **SSVEP** — targets flicker at distinct frequencies; the occipital response is matched to the
  flicker. The best-performing family by a distance. The published state of the art reached about
  **5.3 bits/s** with 40 targets, a trained participant and a good cap **[V]** — treat that as a
  ceiling you will not reach, not a specification.
- **P300 / oddball** — slower, needs averaging over repetitions, but requires no flicker.
- **Motor imagery** — imagining left- versus right-hand movement. **The worst option for a design
  lab**: it needs per-user training across sessions, and a well-documented fraction of people
  (commonly cited at 15–30%) never achieve usable control at all.

**What it cannot do:** decode language, images or free-form thought. A "BCI" here means **selecting
among a small set of predefined on-screen targets, slowly, with errors**.

#### Use it in this course

**The latency, the error rate and the effort *are* the design problem.** For an HCI or interaction
project, a control interface that is slow, unreliable and tiring is not a failed interface — it is a
rich one. What does an interface look like when every selection costs four seconds and fails one time
in five? That question has real design content, and almost nobody addresses it seriously.

**SSVEP as a visual medium.** A flickering interface is a *designed* flickering interface. Frequency,
contrast, spatial arrangement and colour are all yours to choose, and they all affect
classification accuracy. A generative system that lays out SSVEP targets, optimising simultaneously
for legibility and for signal separability, is an unusually well-defined and genuinely novel project.

**Be honest in the wall text.** If a piece claims to respond to a viewer's brain, say what it is
actually responding to.

**Gotchas:** SSVEP flicker is fatiguing and carries a **photosensitive-seizure risk** that must be in
your ethics protocol. Occipital electrodes (Oz, O1, O2) sit exactly where the head touches a chair
back — seating matters. And the "edge" classifier is a black box you cannot validate: **insist on
raw-data export**.

#### Learn it

- **BrainFlow** — a uniform API across many EEG boards, with a **Synthetic Board** so you can build

  <https://brainflow.readthedocs.io/>
- **OpenViBE** — visual dataflow for real-time BCI, with native OSC output —

### Portable Physiological Human Factors Data Collector

**Vendor: 北京恒挚 · Qty 5** *(spreadsheet)*

**Model unresolved [?].** Either five units of the same wearable as the recorder above, or a
multi-channel portable physiology amplifier in the class of a BioRadio or BIOPAC SmartCenter — both
of which Everloyal distributes. **Five units strongly implies parallel data collection**: five
participants at once, or five student teams. That is a teaching-lab configuration, and it is the most
useful fact about this item.

#### What it is

A portable multi-channel physiological amplifier. The standard channel set for human-factors work,
with the sampling rates that actually matter:

| Signal | Unit | Typical rate | What it indexes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **EDA / GSR** | µS | 4–32 Hz is ample | sympathetic arousal |
| **ECG** | mV | 250–1000 Hz | cardiac timing → HRV |
| **PPG / BVP** | arbitrary | 64–256 Hz | pulse, peripheral blood volume |
| **Respiration** | arbitrary (belt) | 25–100 Hz | breathing rate and depth |
| **Skin temperature** | °C | 1–4 Hz | peripheral vasoconstriction |
| **EMG** | µV | 1000+ Hz | muscle activation, including facial |
| **Accelerometry** | g | 50–100 Hz | movement, and artefact flagging |

**Why the rates differ so much:** EDA is a slow signal with essentially no content above 5 Hz, so
32 Hz is generous. ECG needs a high rate not to see the waveform but to time the **R-peak
precisely** — HRV is computed from millisecond differences between beats, so 250 Hz gives you 4 ms
resolution and 1000 Hz gives you 1 ms. Under-sampling ECG silently inflates your HRV noise floor,
and nothing in the output tells you it happened.

#### Use it in this course

**Five units is the interesting number.** It lets you do something most design-research projects
cannot: record **several people simultaneously** during a shared activity. A group critique, a
collaborative build, an audience watching the same work. Synchrony between participants' arousal is
a measurable thing, and it is a far better question for a design course than individual emotion
detection.

**Teams, not individuals.** Five collectors means five groups running parallel pilots in one
session. If a biosignal exercise happens this term, this is what makes it feasible at cohort scale.

**Gotchas:** every one of these signals needs a **within-participant baseline** and is meaningless
in absolute terms across people. Electrode placement changes the signal more than the phenomenon
does. Respiration confounds HRV — deep breaths drive high-frequency power — so log breathing if you
intend to interpret HRV at all.

### Portable Electrodermal Activity (EDA) Data Acquisition System

**Listed vendor: Shimmer · Qty 3** *(spreadsheet)* — **the one item in this room with real,
citable specifications.**

#### What it is

The **Shimmer3 GSR+**: a small wireless unit measuring **skin conductance in microsiemens**, with
an optional optical pulse probe for PPG and a full 10-degree-of-freedom IMU.

**Specs [V]:** GSR range **10 kΩ – 4.7 MΩ (0.2–100 µS) ±10%**, or 22 k–680 kΩ (1.5–45 µS) ±3% ·
frequency range **DC–15.9 Hz** · **450 mAh** battery · **8 GB microSD** for onboard logging ·
**65 × 32 × 12 mm, 28 g** · Bluetooth, about 10 m indoors · wide-range accelerometer ±2/4/8/16 g,
gyroscope, magnetometer.

#### What EDA actually is

Skin conductance driven by eccrine sweat gland activity, which is innervated **purely by the
sympathetic branch** of the autonomic nervous system. That single fact is why this is the most
trustworthy signal in the lab: there is no parasympathetic confound to disentangle.

Two components, and you need both:

- **SCL, the tonic level** — a slow baseline that drifts over minutes and varies enormously between
  people. A 2 µS person and a 20 µS person are both entirely normal. **Never compare raw SCL across
  participants.**
- **SCR, the phasic responses** — discrete 0.5–5 µS bumps, onset **1–4 seconds after** a stimulus,
  rising over 1–3 s and recovering over 2–10 s. That latency is a hard physiological floor: **EDA
  cannot resolve events closer together than a few seconds.** For rapid stimulus sequences, use EEG.

#### The three settings that decide whether your data is any good

1. **Fix the range; do not use auto-range.** Shimmer explicitly warn against auto-ranging at high
   sampling rates — the range-switching settling time creates step artefacts that look *exactly*
   like SCRs. Settings 2 (220–680 kΩ) and 3 (680 kΩ–4.7 MΩ) cover typical tonic skin conductance.
   Pick one, verify it covers your participants, leave it. **[V]** This is the number-one Shimmer
   mistake.
2. **Sample at 32–128 Hz, not 512.** The user guide recommends **0–5 Hz for tonic and 0.03–5 Hz for
   phasic** — EDA has no meaningful content above about 5 Hz. Sampling faster gives you a hundred
   times the file size, the same information and more noise. Low-pass filter at 1–5 Hz. **[V]**
3. **Placement:** Ag/AgCl electrodes, 1 cm², on the **palmar surface of the medial phalanx of one
   finger and the distal phalanx of an adjacent finger** — index and middle conventionally, on the
   non-dominant hand so the participant can still use a mouse **[V]**. Do **not** clean the site with
   alcohol; it dehydrates the stratum corneum and depresses conductance. Plain water, or nothing.

#### Use it in this course

**This is where to start if you want a body in your system.** It is small, it is cheap to run, the
signal is real, and three units means parallel work. Compared to EEG it is trivially easy — no cap,
no impedance check, no hair washing.

**Arousal as a live parameter.** Skin conductance driving a generative system in real time is the
most achievable biosignal installation in this lab. Smooth it hard — a 2–10 second window and a
per-participant baseline — because raw EDA driving anything visible produces an installation that
responds to nothing legible.

**The honest version.** Show the participant their own signal. An interface that reflects your
arousal back at you, with the one-to-four-second delay visible, is a better piece than one that
claims to know how you feel.

**Pair it with the eye tracker.** Where someone looked, plus how aroused they were, time-aligned, is
a genuinely strong combination and is what the multi-device sync section below is for.

**Skill level:** low to intermediate. Recording something on day one is realistic.

#### Getting started

**ConsensysPRO** is Shimmer's own configuration and logging software (licensed separately). For
everything else there are official Java/Android, **Python**, C#, LabVIEW and MATLAB APIs, plus the
community `pyshimmer` library. **NeuroKit2**'s `eda_process` handles tonic/phasic decomposition and
SCR detection in a few lines.

**Gotchas:** rubbing the Velcro straps together moves the electrodes and injects noise — tape the
leads down **[V]**. Allow **5–10 minutes of acclimatisation** before recording anything you intend
to interpret. Room temperature drives sweating, so a warm studio inflates SCL — log it. And note the
Shimmer3 GSR+ is **discontinued**, superseded by the Shimmer3R: buy spare electrodes, straps and
optical probes now rather than in three years.

#### The five things to establish before you plan a study

1. **Raw data export in an open format** — EDF/BDF at minimum, ideally direct LSL. A system that
   only exports through its own GUI is a system whose data you will lose.
2. **The electrode montage map** — which 10–20 positions, and where reference and ground sit. A
   "32-channel" cap with a proprietary layout is much less useful than one at standard sites.
3. **Whether the reference is linked-mastoid, Cz, or CMS/DRL.** This determines every subsequent
   analysis choice.
4. **A working impedance-check mode.** Without it you cannot do quantitative EEG at all.
5. **Documented event-marker input** — TTL, parallel port, or an LSL marker stream. Without
   sub-10-millisecond event marking, ERP work is impossible.

#### Running a session

1. **Consent and screening** (10 min). Scalp condition, hair products. Participants with gel, wax,
   braids, extensions or very thick curly hair are genuinely harder — budget more time, and be
   honest with them in recruitment rather than surprising them on the day.
2. **Head measurement and cap sizing** (5 min). Measure nasion to inion and circumference; mark
   **Cz** at 50% of both. **Wrong cap size means wrong electrode positions — your "Fz" is not Fz**,
   and nothing downstream will tell you.
3. **Electrode preparation** — 5 minutes saline, 25 minutes gel for 32 channels.
4. **Impedance check** (5 min). Never skip it. **Record the impedance values as data** — they belong
   in your methods section.
5. **Artefact calibration** (2–3 min). Eyes open, eyes closed, deliberate blinks, horizontal
   saccades, jaw clench. You need these to build and validate artefact removal later.
6. **Task** — aim for under 45 minutes on saline, under 90 on gel including breaks.
7. **Re-check impedance at the end.** A channel that drifted is a channel you must justify keeping.
8. **Cleanup** — 10 minutes saline, 25 minutes gel plus participant washing.

**Afterwards, run a bridge check.** Two channels with near-zero difference across the whole
recording are bridged, and it is not recoverable in analysis.

#### Use it in this course

**Start with the Synthetic Board.** BrainFlow ships a simulated EEG device. Build your entire
pipeline — acquisition, filtering, feature extraction, the thing it drives — against simulated data
before you ever put a cap on a person. You will find every bug in your code while the ethics
application is still in review, and when a real participant is sitting in the chair you will be
debugging physiology rather than sockets. This is the single best move available here.

**Alpha is the one signal you can rely on.** Posterior alpha rises reliably when eyes close. A piece
driven by eyes-open/eyes-closed is trivial physiologically and completely legible to an audience —
which makes it a much better first installation than an "emotion detector" that does not work.

**Make the setup visible.** The twenty-five minutes of gel injection, the impedance map, the hair
washing — the apparatus of measurement is a subject in its own right, and for a course thinking about
what it means for a system to observe a person, the ritual around the instrument is at least as
interesting as its output.

**Scope realistically.** One EEG participant is roughly two hours of your life. A study with n=20 is
a term's work on its own. If EEG is a component of your project rather than the whole of it, plan for
n=3 as a demonstration and say so honestly.

#### Learn it

  <https://mne.tools/stable/auto_tutorials/index.html>

### The other instruments in DZ 15-101

Four more measurement instruments and three bench instruments, covered more briefly. All appear on
both the website and the spreadsheet.

**Wearable Gaze Interaction Device for Spatial Design** *(北京恒挚, ×1)* — eye-tracking glasses.
Probable match **EveVision G120**: one-point calibration, forward-facing HD scene camera, CSV raw
export, analysis software with heatmaps, gaze paths and areas of interest, and an SDK exposing gaze
intersection point, **blink data and pupil diameter** **[V]**. Sampling rate and accuracy are
unpublished — the "120" *suggests* 120 Hz but do not report that as fact **[?]**. For comparison, the
class benchmark Tobii Pro Glasses 3 runs 50 or 100 Hz at 0.6° accuracy.

*This is the best instrument in the lab for spatial design research* — wayfinding, signage, display
attention, architectural legibility. Three warnings: **slippage** is the chronic failure (recalibrate
periodically and validate at the end); **sunlight floods the infrared** and breaks tracking; and
**coding the scene video is the real cost** — a ten-minute walkthrough can take hours to code, and
that, not battery life, determines how many participants you can run. Budget for **10–20% of
participants being untrackable**; that is normal and reporting it is part of doing the method
honestly. Pupil diameter is dominated by ambient light, so pupillometry as a cognitive-load measure
is unusable outdoors.

**Visual Behavior Testing Platform for Smart Mobile Interfaces** *(HTC VIVE Focus Vision, ×1)* — a
VR headset with **integrated 120 Hz binocular eye tracking, 0.5–1.1° accuracy within a 20° field,
five-point calibration**, 2448 × 2448 per eye, up to 120° field of view, ~2 hour hot-swappable
battery **[V]**. Note the accuracy caveat: it degrades outside that 20° cone, so peripheral-attention
research is a stretch.

The method the name describes is exact: **render a mobile UI on a virtual screen inside VR and
measure where the user looks.** You get perfect stimulus control and **ground-truth gaze with no
AOI-coding cost**, because you own the scene graph and know exactly what the ray hit. Luminance is
controllable, so pupillometry actually works here. The open question is ecological validity —
looking at a simulated phone is not looking at a phone, and whether findings transfer is a claim you
have to defend.

**Use VIVE OpenXR, not SRanipal** — the legacy SDK is deprecated. The **VIVE XR Eye Tracker feature
(Beta)**, requiring VIVE OpenXR Plugin 2.5.0 or newer, gives gaze pose, **pupil diameter** and
**eye openness/squeeze/wide** **[V]**; the portable `XR_EXT_eye_gaze_interaction` standard gives you
a gaze ray and nothing more. Version-pin the plugin — it is Beta. Face gaskets are shared
skin-contact items; disposable covers and a cleaning procedure go in the ethics protocol.

**Design Visual Perception Experimental Apparatus** *(北京恒挚, ×5)* — **the vaguest item in the
inventory** and the one most likely to be misdescribed if you guess. Two readings, both plausible:
five **screen-based eye-tracking workstations** (Everloyal sells EveVision D60/D120 remote binocular
trackers, and five parallel stations is a teaching-lab configuration), or a **psychophysics
stimulus-presentation rig** — calibrated monitor, chinrest, response box — for classical perception
measurement. **Check the physical units: if they have infrared illuminators and cameras, it is the
former.** **[?]**

If eye tracking: strictly better than glasses for anything on a screen — higher precision, no
slippage, and no AOI coding because the software knows the stimulus coordinates. If psychophysics:
the instrument is really the software plus a calibrated display, and the essential, usually skipped
step is **monitor gamma calibration with a photometer** — without it, "contrast" and "luminance" in
your write-up are not the values you think they are.

Either way the software is **PsychoPy** (<https://www.psychopy.org/>) — free, Python, GUI builder
plus code, precise timing, and it emits LSL event markers so a perception task on one of these five
stations can be time-locked to the EEG cap. Psychtoolbox-3 is the MATLAB alternative. Watch your
timing floor: a 60 Hz monitor quantises stimulus onset to 16.7 ms, which matters for ERPs; and fix
viewing distance with a chinrest or your degrees of visual angle are fiction.

**Finger Trajectory Analyzer for Interactive Prototypes** *(北京恒挚, ×1)* — no matching product in
the vendor's public catalogue **[?]**, but the *method* is well established and this is the most
under-used technique in the room.

It records the **continuous spatial trajectory** of a finger during an interaction, not just where
it landed. This is the hardware form of **mouse-tracking**, introduced by Freeman & Ambady's
MouseTracker (2010) and now best served by the open-source **`mousetrap`** R package. The core
insight: while a participant moves toward a choice, the trajectory **curves toward the alternative
they did not pick**, and the curvature indexes decision conflict in real time. A reaction time gives
you one number; a trajectory gives you a continuous readout of hesitation.

Standard measures, all computed by `mousetrap`: **MAD** (maximum deviation from the straight line),
**AUC**, **x-flips** (direction reversals, a hesitation count), trajectory entropy, and
time-normalised trajectories for averaging across trials.

**For design work this is genuinely valuable**: which of two menu labels competes with which, where
in a flow users hesitate, whether a redesign reduces conflict, comparing icon sets by curvature
rather than by questionnaire.

**Two traps.** First, **task design determines whether you get anything at all** — the paradigm
requires the participant to *start moving before deciding*. If they can deliberate with a stationary
finger and then tap, every trajectory is a straight line carrying no information. The standard fix is
to require movement initiation within about 400 ms. Second, **average curvature can be an artefact of
averaging**: a mixture of straight trials and abrupt mid-flight corrections averages to a smooth
curve that no individual trial resembled. Check trial-level distributions before interpreting a mean.

- `mousetrap` R package — <https://github.com/PascalKieslich/mousetrap>
- Kieslich & Henninger (2017), *Behavior Research Methods* — <https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13428-017-0900-z>
- Freeman & Ambady (2010), the origin paper — <https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BRM.42.1.226>

**Oscilloscope** *(Tektronix MDO3014)*, **high-precision multimeter** *(Keithley DAQ6510)* and
**electrometer** *(Keithley 6517B)* — proper electronics bench instruments, and better ones than most
design schools have. The **electrometer** is the interesting one: it measures currents down to the
femtoamp range, which is what you need to characterise **triboelectric and piezoelectric materials,
capacitive sensing and e-textiles**. If your project involves making your own sensor — conductive
thread, pressure-sensitive fabric, a hand-built capacitive surface — this is the instrument that
tells you whether it works. The oscilloscope is what you reach for when a microcontroller project
behaves inexplicably.

### Getting biosignals into your own work

This section is the practical core of everything above, and it is the same for every instrument in
the room.

**The reliable pattern is: device → LSL → bridge → OSC → creative tool.** Do not try to talk to the
hardware directly from Max or TouchDesigner; you will spend the project on serial protocols.

**Lab Streaming Layer (LSL)** is the lingua franca — a network protocol that handles multi-device
streaming *and* time synchronisation, which is the hard part. **LabRecorder** captures all streams
into a single `.xdf` file. If you record EEG, EDA and VR gaze into one XDF, they are time-aligned and
you can ask questions that span them. If you record three CSVs with three clocks, you cannot.

| Tool | Role |
|---|---|
| **Lab Streaming Layer** | Multi-device streaming and time sync — <https://labstreaminglayer.readthedocs.io/> |
| **LabRecorder** | Records all LSL streams to one `.xdf` |
| **BrainFlow** | Uniform API across many EEG boards, plus a Synthetic Board for hardware-free development — <https://brainflow.readthedocs.io/> |
| **MNE-Python** | Reference EEG analysis — <https://mne.tools/stable/> |
| **NeuroKit2** | EDA, ECG, PPG, respiration, EMG in Python — <https://neuropsychology.github.io/NeuroKit/> |
| **OpenViBE** | Real-time BCI dataflow with native OSC output — <http://openvibe.inria.fr/> |

**Bridging to creative tools:**

- **Python bridge** — `pylsl` in, `python-osc` out. About thirty lines. **This is the recommended
  path for this course**, because it forces you to write the filtering and feature extraction
  explicitly rather than accepting a black box, which is exactly the kind of understanding this
  course is asking for.
- **TouchDesigner** — OSC In CHOP.
- **Max/MSP** — `udpreceive` plus `OSC-route`, or the `odot` library.
- **Unity** — LSL4Unity, or OSC via extOSC / OscJack. A short Unity script publishing VR gaze and
  pupil over LSL lets you align headset data with the EEG and the Shimmer in one file. **This is the
  highest-value integration available in this lab.**
- **p5.js** — browsers cannot open raw sockets, so run a small Node or Python WebSocket relay that
  subscribes to LSL and pushes JSON to the sketch. Processing can use `oscP5` directly.

**The warning that saves installations:** smooth aggressively before the signal drives anything
visible. Raw EEG band power updates 10–30 times a second and is dominated by artefact. An
installation driven by unsmoothed alpha is an installation driven by the participant's blinks. Use a
2–10 second moving window and a per-participant baseline — and say in the wall text what the piece is
actually responding to.

**"I want to scan the world and put it back changed."**
3D scanner (13-103) → the i9 workstation (15-102) for meshing → FDM or resin (13-103) to print it
back.

**"I want to make an edition or a series."**
Screen printer (13-103) for prints on anything; large-format plotter (13-103 or 15-201) for paper at
A1; FDM print farm (13-103) for a parameter sweep in three dimensions.

**"I want to make a spatial or projected piece."**
4K projector and VR headsets (15-201), plus the workstations (15-102) to drive them. The lowest
fabrication overhead of any route here.
