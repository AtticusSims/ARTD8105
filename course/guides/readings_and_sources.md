## The big change: ACM is now fully open access

**As of 1 January 2026 the entire ACM Digital Library is free to read for anyone, including the backfile.** ACM's own library-relations pages state that the DL contents are "free to access without a subscription on a basic version of the Digital Library," and that DL Basic provides "PDF versions of all ACM published articles."

That matters here because the methodology core of this course — the CHI and TOCHI papers from 2007 and 2012 — sat in the closed 2001–2025 window until this year. It is now open.

Two caveats worth knowing:

- **Free to read is not the same as openly licensed.** ACM has not stated that pre-2026 articles were retroactively given Creative Commons licences. You may read and download freely. For anything *redistributed* — a shared course pack, a bulk corpus — prefer the repository copies listed below, which carry explicit deposit terms.
- **Bulk downloading is a Premium feature.** One-at-a-time PDF downloads are free. Scripted harvesting of many papers is not. UM's ACM Open membership should cover Premium if you need it; ask the Library.

## Using readings as Cursor context

Once you have PDFs in a `readings/` folder, they become part of your working context.

```
@readings/frayling_1993.pdf @readings/candy_2006.pdf

I'm deciding whether my project is practice-based or practice-led.
Using these two sources specifically — not general knowledge — explain the
distinction and tell me which mine appears to be. Quote the passages you
rely on.
```

Two habits that make this work:

**Insist it quotes.** An agent asked about a text will happily answer from its training rather than from the file in front of it. Requiring quotations forces it to actually read.

**Prefer the PDFs over its memory.** Your agent has vague, averaged impressions of these papers. The PDF is the paper. When they disagree, the PDF is right — and if the agent tells you a paper says something you cannot find in it, that is worth noticing.

## Three rules about sources

**Only legitimate sources.** Shadow libraries — Sci-Hub, LibGen, Anna's Archive, Z-Library — are out. Not primarily as a moral point: you are about to submit work to venues whose reviewers care about this, and citing something you obtained improperly is a bad habit to carry into a research career. Everything on this list is either free or available through the Library.

**Random PDFs on course websites are not a source.** A scan of a copyrighted book on some university's server is usually an unauthorised upload. Use it to check whether a book is worth ordering if you like; do not build on it.

**Verify every citation before it enters your writing.** Open the DOI. Confirm the title, authors, year, venue and page numbers. Even this list has one discrepancy in it — the Bishop page range — and I flagged it because I checked. Two of the three "required readings" in this course's own accreditation document turned out not to exist at all.

## Building your own list

The seven methodology readings and four critical readings above are the shared canon. **From session 6 your personal reading stream converges on your own topic**, and you find those yourself.

```
I'm working on [topic] and targeting [venue].
Find 10 recent papers I should read, prioritising:
- work published at my target venue in the last three years
- work that would be an obvious omission if I didn't cite it
Tag every one VERIFIED or UNVERIFIED, and tell me which are open access
and which need the Library.
```

Then check the unverified ones yourself, and write a reading note for each using `course/templates/reading_note.md`.
