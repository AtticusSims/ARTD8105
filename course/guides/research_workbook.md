## How to use this

Each step has a **named output** — a file in your workspace. The eleven outputs are the spine of your term.

Each step carries **💬 USE THE AGENT** blocks: worked starting prompts with `[bracketed slots]` for your own content. They are starting points. Adapt them, and expect to iterate three or four times per block.

**The agent is a research partner, not a decision-maker.** It is strong at finding, checking, structuring and critiquing. It is weak at knowing what matters, at judging your field, and at anything requiring taste. Everything it produces about the world needs verifying. Everything it produces about your work needs deciding on.

**Load `research_agent.md` at the start of every session.** Read it once yourself. It tells the agent what it may and may not do, and you should know what you are entitled to expect.

# STEP 1 — Territory scan
**Sessions 2–3 · Output: `territory_map.md` + reading notes**

**Read before you propose anything.** This is the step most people skip and it is the one that determines whether your project is a contribution or a reinvention. In research, novelty is *defined* by the literature — so the literature has to come first, not as validation afterwards.

You are not looking for your topic yet. You are learning what the field is currently arguing about.

### Do

1. Read **six papers** this fortnight. Two from the course reading list, four you find yourself.
2. Write a **reading note** for each using `templates/reading_note.md`.
3. Build `territory_map.md`: the sub-conversations you can identify, who is in each, what they disagree about, and where the arguments seem to be going.

### 💬 USE THE AGENT

```
I'm mapping the field of [computational art / digital fabrication /
practice-led design research] before choosing a topic.

Give me the 6-8 distinct sub-conversations currently active in this area.
For each: what is being argued, 2-3 representative papers or authors, and
what a newcomer is likely to misunderstand about it.

Tag every citation VERIFIED or UNVERIFIED. I will check the unverified ones.
```

```
Here are the six papers I've read: [list].
What are they collectively NOT talking about? Where are the silences?
```

> **The agent will try to help you pick a topic. Do not let it.** If it proposes one, note it in `territory_map.md` under "premature ideas" and keep reading. Ideas that survive two weeks of reading are worth more than ideas that arrive in week one.

### Reading notes are structured files, not slides

Use the markdown template with YAML front matter, one file per paper in `reading_notes/`. This matters: by session 10 you will have twenty of them, and the agent can read the whole set at once. That is how your related-work section gets written from evidence rather than memory.

**Output check:** six reading notes, and a territory map naming at least five sub-conversations.

# STEP 2 — Position map
**Session 4 · Output: `position_map.md`**

Three lists, then their intersections.

- **What I can do** — methods, tools, materials, access. Include the course stack and what you already bring.
- **What I care about** — problems, phenomena, sites, questions that hold your attention
- **What the field is arguing about** — from Step 1, in your own words

### 💬 USE THE AGENT

```
Generate 20 methods available within this course's stack (p5.js, three.js,
MediaPipe, ml5.js, Blender/Geometry Nodes/bpy, 3D printing, laser cutting,
pen plotting, photogrammetry). For each, name one thing it makes possible
that is hard by other means.
```

```
From my territory map [paste], list 15 open questions that a made artifact
could plausibly answer. Not questions requiring a large user study.
Mark which sub-conversation each belongs to.
```

```
Macau-specific: list 10 sites, materials, practices or conditions here that
are under-documented in the English-language research literature and that a
computational or fabrication method could engage.
```

**Output check:** three lists, each with at least ten items, plus a short note on which intersections feel alive.

# STEP 3 — Candidate questions
**Session 5 · Output: `candidate_questions.md`**

Write **6–10** candidates in this form:

> *What happens to **[PHENOMENON]** when **[METHOD or MATERIAL]** is used to **[ACTION]**, and what does that reveal about **[LARGER CONCERN]**?*

The form is a scaffold, not a straitjacket — but keep all four slots. The fourth is what separates a research question from a project brief.

**Two things to resist.** Don't optimise for volume; six good candidates beat fifteen thin ones. And don't discard the ones that feel too small — small and answerable beats large and gestural, every time, for a first paper.

### 💬 USE THE AGENT

```
Here is my position map [paste]. Generate 10 more candidate questions in
this form: [form]. Prioritise combinations I appear not to have considered.
For each, name which of my three lists it draws on.
```

Then, separately:

```
For each of my candidate questions, answer only this:
Could this be answered by making something, within 10 weeks, by one person
with this stack? Answer yes / no / partly, and for "partly" say what would
have to be cut. Do not rank them yet.
```

**Output check:** 6–10 candidates, each answerable by making.

# STEP 5 — Literature verification sprint
**Built in the 30 September week, due session 7 · Output: `literature_verification.md`**

For your two or three strongest questions, establish what is genuinely known.

For each question, produce:
- **What is already established** — with verified citations
- **What has been tried and failed** — often the most useful and hardest to find
- **The gap**, stated in one sentence
- **The five works your paper will have to engage**, with a critique of each
- **A verdict**: green / amber / red

**Red means the question is already answered.** That is a good outcome for this step, not a bad one — you found out in session 7 rather than session 14.

### 💬 USE THE AGENT

```
Question: [paste].
Search for prior work that answers or partly answers this. Be adversarial —
your job is to find the paper that makes my question redundant, not to
reassure me. Report what you find with VERIFIED tags and give me a
green/amber/red verdict with reasoning.
```

```
For these five works [list], write one critique each: what does it do, what
does it not do, and what would my project add? Not a summary — a critique.
```

> **Verify every citation yourself before it enters this file.** Open the DOI. Check the title, authors, year and venue. Two of the three "required readings" in this course's own accreditation document do not exist — that is what unverified citations look like when they survive into an official document.

**Output check:** a verdict per question, and at least one question you killed.

# STEP 6 — Commit and declare
**Due session 7 · 7 October · Output: `idea_evaluation.md` — ASSESSED**

Pick one question. Complete `templates/idea_evaluation.md`.

This template is adapted from Wu Ziwei's Idea Evaluation Template and covers: the narrowed problem, whether it is new or old, your method and its novelty, your three contributions, related work with critiques, expected results, discussion, a full figure list, and remaining tasks with milestones.

Two additions specific to this course:

**Declare your contribution type** — existence proof, method contribution, annotated portfolio, intermediate concept, critical/speculative, or empirical. The type determines what evidence you need. See `research_agent.md` §7. If you choose **empirical**, you need ethics approval and you need to start it now.

**Record your venue constraints** — word limit, format, anonymisation, deadline, from your Step 4 file. These constrain everything downstream; a 2,500-word short paper and a 12-page pictorial are different arguments, not the same argument at different lengths.

### The necessity test

Ask the agent, and answer honestly:

```
Apply the necessity test to my project:
1. Remove the making — is the finding still supported? If yes, why make anything?
2. Remove the domain — is the method still a contribution? If yes, why this site?
Both must fail for the project to be coherent. Tell me which one is weak.
```

**Output check:** completed idea evaluation, contribution type declared, necessity test passed.

# STEP 7 — Claim construction
**Session 8 · Output: `claim.md`**

Write these four sentences. Expect the first attempt to be bad.

1. **I made** ———
2. **In making it I found that** ———
3. **Which means that** ———
4. **Which is useful to** ———

Sentence 3 is the whole game. It must be true of more than your project, and it must be **contestable** — a reasonable person should be able to disagree.

### 💬 USE THE AGENT

```
Here are my four sentences [paste]. Attack sentence 3 specifically.
- What would have to be true for it to be FALSE?
- If nothing could falsify it, say so — it isn't a claim.
- Is it true of anything beyond my own project? If not, what would make it so?
Do not rewrite my sentences. Tell me where they fail.
```

Rewrite `claim.md` at the end of every subsequent step. Keep every version in the file, dated. The drift is data — how your claim changed as you made things is one of the most publishable things you will have.

**Output check:** four sentences, plus a note on what a skeptic would say.

# STEP 8 — Make, and log the making
**Sessions 8–11 · Output: artifacts, process journal, agent log**

Sustained practice work, alongside the fabrication track.

**The process journal is data, not a diary.** Record decisions and rejected alternatives; failures in detail — what you expected, what happened, what you think caused the gap; surprises; constraints discovered; direction changes; and numbers — iterations, settings, print times, failure counts.

Do not record effort or mood. "I struggled for days" tells a reader nothing. *What* you struggled against tells them a great deal.

**The agent log runs automatically** — see `03_Provenance_Logging_Spec.md`. Review the weekly summary and file corrections. That review is what makes the log evidence rather than exhaust.

### 💬 USE THE AGENT

```
[When something fails]
Here's what I expected: [X]. Here's what happened: [Y].
Before we fix it: what does this failure reveal about the constraints of
[material / tool / method]? Write it as a decision record if it's substantive.
```

```
[Weekly]
Read this week's session logs and my process journal. What recurring tension
do you see that I haven't named? Quote the entries you're drawing on.
```

**The second prompt is where findings come from.** Run it every week from session 9.

**Output check:** weekly journal entries, weekly log summaries signed off, at least four decision records by session 11.

# STEP 9 — Structure and draft
**Session 11 · 4 November · Output: `draft_v1.md` + figure plan**

### Analyse before you write

```
@venue/<slug>.md
Using the section proportions in my venue file, give me a target word count
per section for my paper. Then tell me which section I have the least
material for.
```

### Figure plan first

List every figure before writing prose: number, content, purpose, status. Figures carry more of a practice-led argument than authors expect, and planning them first tells you what evidence you are missing while there is still time to make it.

### Then write

**You write the draft.** The agent may critique structure, check proportions against the venue file, fix grammar and idiom, and tell you where an argument does not land. It may not write your paragraphs. Text it offers is marked `[AGENT DRAFT]` and exists to be reacted to, not pasted.

### Mine your own record

```
Read my process journal and the decision records. Give me the 5 moments
that best evidence my claim, with dates and quotes. For each, say which
section of the paper it belongs in.
```

**Output check:** complete draft at the venue's word count, full figure plan, every claim traceable to journal or artifact.

# STEP 10 — Mock review
**Session 12 · 11 November — ASSESSED**

Two parts.

**Ranking.** Everyone presents; everyone ranks all other presenters on Presentation Format, Academic Contribution, and Personal Learning, using `templates/peer_review.md`. Fast, and it forces discrimination.

**Paired deep review.** You are assigned one peer's draft and you review it **using the actual review form from their target venue**, from their venue file. You produce a written review in that venue's format.

Reviewing is the strongest learning moment in this course. You will understand your own paper better after attacking someone else's than after any amount of advice about writing.

### 💬 USE THE AGENT

```
@venue/<their-slug>.md
I'm reviewing a peer's draft using their venue's review form. Help me apply
each criterion rigorously — for each, tell me what evidence in the draft
would satisfy it. Do not write my review. I'll draft it, then you tell me
where I'm being vague or unfair.
```

Then, on the reviews you receive:

```
Here are the reviews I got [paste]. Sort them into:
(a) must fix — the paper is wrong or unclear
(b) should fix — the paper is weaker without it
(c) reviewer's taste — legitimate but optional
Justify each placement. I'll decide.
```

**Output check:** one written review delivered in your peer's venue format; a triaged response plan for your own.

### The AI disclosure

Write it yourself, from your actual log. Something in this shape, adapted to your venue's policy and your real practice:

> AI assistance was used as follows. Literature search and triage: candidate sources were identified with [tool], and every cited claim was verified against the source by the author. Code: implementation was AI-assisted under the author's direction; the author is able to explain and modify all submitted code. Text: the argument, structure and prose are the author's; AI assistance was limited to grammar, idiom and structural feedback. A complete interaction log covering [N] sessions is retained by the author.

**That last sentence is one almost nobody can write.** You can. Say so.

---

## The four rules

1. **Never submit AI-generated text as your own writing.** Grammar and idiom help is fine. The argument is yours.
2. **Verify every citation before it enters a document.** No exceptions.
3. **Be able to explain everything you submit** — every line of code, every sentence.
4. **Log as you go.** Reconstructed later is not the same thing, and the difference is visible.
