## Before anything else

You already know how to make things. That is not what this course is assessing, and it is not what a journal or conference will publish.

The gap between a strong portfolio and a publishable paper is not quality of execution. It is that a portfolio shows **what you made** and a paper argues **what is now known that was not known before**, in a form that lets someone else use it. A beautiful object with no claim attached is not research. A modest object with a clear, evidenced, transferable claim is.

This guide is about closing that gap. Most of it is about one move: **from "I made this" to "this demonstrates X."**

A note on where you're starting from. Your Year 1 methods training came from an art-historical tradition — research as the interpretation of existing objects and documents. That is a real and rigorous method, and it is not the one you need here. In practice-led research **you make the object you then reason from**. The evidence does not exist until you produce it. That changes what counts as rigour, what counts as data, and what you have to record while you work. If some of what follows contradicts what you were taught last year, that is expected.

## 1. Three kinds of research, and which one you are doing

Christopher Frayling's 1993 distinction is still the clearest starting point.

**Research *into* art and design** — historical, theoretical, critical. You study objects, movements, technologies. The art-historical mode.

**Research *through* art and design** — the making is the inquiry. You produce something in order to find out something, and the knowledge emerges from the process and the object together.

**Research *for* art and design** — you gather knowledge in order to make a better thing. The end point is the artefact itself.

Almost every designer's instinct is research *for*. It is what professional practice is: you research the client, the market, the material, so the product works. **It is also the mode that does not publish**, because the output is the artefact and the knowledge stays with you.

A DDes lives in research *through*. The test is simple: **does someone who never sees your object gain something usable from your account of making it?** If yes, you have research. If no, you have a project.

### Practice-based or practice-led

Linda Candy's distinction, which you will be asked about:

- **Practice-based** — the creative artefact is the *basis* of the contribution. The knowledge is partly in the thing, and the thing must be experienced for the claim to be fully understood.
- **Practice-led** — the practice *leads* to insights about practice itself. The contribution is operational knowledge; the artefact is evidence for it.

Most work in this course will be practice-led with a practice-based component. You do not need to pick a camp, but you do need to know which one your paper's claim rests on, because it determines whether the artefact must be exhibited alongside the text.

## 2. The central problem: "I made this" is not a claim

Here is the move, in four steps. Write these four sentences about your own project from session 5, badly, and rewrite them every few weeks.

1. **I made** ——— *(the artefact, described factually)*
2. **In making it I found that** ——— *(the observation — something that surprised you, resisted you, or turned out otherwise than expected)*
3. **Which means that** ——— *(the claim — a general statement that extends beyond your object)*
4. **Which is useful to** ——— *(who can act on it, and how)*

Step 3 is where papers are won and lost. Steps 1, 2 and 4 are usually easy for designers. Step 3 requires you to say something that is *true of more than your own project* — and to be able to defend it.

### Weak and strong, worked

**Weak:** "I made a generative type system in p5.js that produces posters from a rule set. The results were visually interesting and I explored many variations."

Nothing here is a claim. "Visually interesting" is taste. "Explored many variations" is an activity. A reviewer has no way to agree or disagree.

**Stronger:** "I made a generative type system in p5.js in which the rule set is authored but the letterform outcomes are not previewable. Working with it, I found that my editorial judgement shifted from selecting outcomes to constraining the space of outcomes — and that I could not evaluate a rule set without generating at least ~40 instances. This suggests that generative authorship relocates the designer's critical act from *choosing* to *bounding*, and that tools for generative typography should therefore prioritise rapid population-level preview over single-instance refinement. That is actionable for anyone building or teaching with such tools."

The second version is the same project. What changed is that an observation was isolated, generalised into a claim about generative authorship, and pointed at someone who could use it. The "~40 instances" matters — it is a specific, falsifiable detail from the process journal, and it is the kind of thing reviewers trust.

### A second example, from fabrication

**Weak:** "I scanned architectural details from Macau shopfront signage, transformed them algorithmically, and 3D printed the results. The prints preserve the character of the originals while introducing digital artefacts."

**Stronger:** "I photogrammetrically scanned six Macau shopfront signs, applied a mesh-decimation transformation, and printed the results at 1:10. Across all six, the algorithmic step destroyed exactly the features that made each sign legible as *Macanese* — the Portuguese-Chinese letterform pairing survived, but the hand-painted edge irregularity did not, because decimation reads irregularity as noise. This is a specific failure of reality-capture pipelines as heritage tools: they preserve geometry and discard the traces of hand production, which for vernacular signage is where cultural specificity actually lives. Anyone using scanning for heritage documentation needs a capture strategy that treats surface irregularity as signal rather than error."

Again: same project. The difference is that a *failure* was treated as the finding, generalised, and aimed at a community that can use it. Note also that this claim is contestable — someone could disagree, or test it on different signage. **Contestability is a feature.** A claim nobody could dispute is usually not a claim.

## 3. What kind of contribution are you making?

Practice research produces several distinct kinds of contribution. Naming yours makes the paper dramatically easier to write, because it tells you what evidence you need.

**Existence proof.** "This is possible, and here is the thing that proves it." The artefact demonstrates a capability, technique or combination that had not been shown. Evidence needed: the artefact, documented well enough that its properties are verifiable.

**Method contribution.** "Here is a way of working that others can adopt." Evidence needed: a clear enough account of the process that someone could follow it, plus some indication that it worked better than the obvious alternative.

**Annotated portfolio.** Rather than one deep case, you present a *set* of related works with analytical annotations, and the knowledge emerges from what they have in common and where they diverge. This suits this course unusually well, because you will produce a series of pieces across the blocks rather than one monolith. Evidence needed: the works, well documented, plus annotations that do analytical work rather than describing.

**Intermediate-level concept.** You name a pattern that sits between "my one object" and "a general theory" — a transferable design idea, illustrated by instances. Evidence needed: your instance, plus at least a gesture toward others in the field that the concept also describes.

**Critical or speculative contribution.** The artefact is an argument — it makes a proposition about technology, culture or practice legible by embodying it. Evidence needed: rigorous positioning against existing critical work, so it reads as a contribution rather than an opinion.

**Empirical contribution.** You made something and then studied how people encountered it. Evidence needed: an actual study design, consent, and analysis. **Only choose this if you plan for it from session 5** — you cannot retrofit ethics approval or participant data.

Most first papers from this cohort should be an **existence proof**, an **annotated portfolio**, or a **method contribution**. Those three require no participants, no ethics process, and no claims you cannot support from your own documentation.

## 4. The process journal is your data

This is the mechanism that makes everything above possible, and it is the thing students most often do badly.

**A process journal is not a diary.** "Today I worked on the mesh for three hours and it was frustrating" is worthless. The journal is a *data collection instrument*, and you are the instrument's subject.

### What to record

Record these, dated, with images or files attached:

- **Decisions, and the alternatives you rejected.** The rejected option is often more informative than the chosen one. Say why.
- **Failures, in detail.** What you expected, what happened, what you think caused the gap. Failures reveal the constraint structure of the material — they are the highest-value entries in the whole journal.
- **Surprises.** Anything that turned out otherwise than expected. Surprise is the signal that your mental model was wrong, which means there is something to learn.
- **Constraints discovered mid-process.** The thing the software would not do; the tolerance the printer could not hold; the file size that broke the browser.
- **Direction changes.** When and why the project became a different project. Note the moment, not just the outcome.
- **Numbers.** How many iterations, how long, how many failed prints, what the settings were. Specifics are what make a claim credible later.

### What to leave out

Effort, mood, and narrative. "I struggled with this for days" tells a reviewer nothing. *What* you struggled against, and what that resistance revealed, tells them a great deal.

### How to analyse it

At session 10 you will read your own journal as evidence, not as memory. Practically:

1. Read the whole thing and mark every entry that records a **surprise, a failure, or a reversal**.
2. Group the marked entries. Recurring tensions will appear — the same conflict showing up in different guises. Those groupings are candidate findings.
3. For each grouping, ask the four-sentence question from §2. Most groupings will die at step 3. One or two will survive.
4. What survives is your paper.

This is why the journal has to be kept from session 1. You cannot reconstruct it, and post-hoc memory is systematically wrong — it smooths out exactly the stumbles that carry the knowledge.

## 5. Documentation is the artefact

Your reviewer will never touch your object. **For them, the documentation *is* the work.** Under-documented practice research fails review not because the work was weak but because the evidence was not there.

Document as you go, not at the end:

- **Process, not just outcome.** Intermediate states, failed attempts, the workspace, the machine mid-print. This material is unrecoverable once you move on.
- **Consistent conditions.** Same lighting, same background, same distance for comparable objects. If you are claiming a difference between six outputs, the photographs must make that difference visible and must not introduce differences of their own.
- **Scale.** Include something that establishes size for physical work. Reviewers routinely misjudge scale.
- **Video where the work is temporal or interactive.** Several venues now *require* it — DIS Interactivity, SIGGRAPH Asia Art Gallery, TEI Art & Performance all mandate a short video. Shoot it properly rather than screen-recording at the deadline.
- **Raw files and code, versioned.** Commit your code with meaningful messages. Your commit history is itself process documentation, and it is the cheapest kind to produce.

**Documentation ≠ illustration.** Illustration makes the work look good. Documentation makes the work legible as evidence. You need both, and they are often different photographs.

## 6. Learn the genre by reading for form

You will read one paper per week from your target venue. Read at least three of them **for structure rather than content**:

- How long is the introduction, and where exactly does the claim first appear?
- How much space goes to describing the artefact versus analysing it? (In good practice papers the ratio surprises people — description is usually shorter than expected.)
- How is the process evidenced? Quotations from a journal? Images? Numbers?
- Where does the paper state what is *transferable*, and in what words?
- What does the related-work section actually do — is it a list, or does it build the gap the paper then fills?

Map two or three papers this way and the genre stops being mysterious. This is also the fastest possible route to writing in it, and it is a far better use of an hour than reading advice about academic writing.

## 7. Anatomy of a practice-led paper

A common structure. Venues vary — the format you must follow comes from the venue's own template, not from here.

| Section | What it does | Common error |
|---|---|---|
| **Title & abstract** | States the claim, not the topic | Naming the artefact instead of the finding |
| **Introduction** | Establishes the gap and states the contribution explicitly | Starting with the project instead of the problem |
| **Related work** | Positions the work; builds the gap the paper fills | A list of everything read, with no argument |
| **The artefact** | What was made, in enough detail to be credible | Too long. This is not the paper's centre. |
| **Process / method** | How you worked, what you recorded, how you analysed it | Omitted entirely — the most common failure |
| **Findings / reflection** | What the making revealed, evidenced from the journal | Description of activity rather than analysis |
| **Discussion** | The transferable claim, and its limits | Overclaiming; or no discussion at all |
| **Limitations & future work** | What this does not show | Treated as a weakness rather than as rigour |

Two observations from reading practice papers that get accepted. First, the **process/method section is where amateur papers give themselves away** — professionals explain how they worked and how they analysed what they recorded; beginners jump straight from artefact to conclusion. Second, **stating limitations makes reviewers trust you more, not less.** A paper that knows its own boundaries reads as authored by someone in control.

## 8. Choose your venue by session 7, and why

Naming the venue is a research act, not an administrative one.

The format determines what argument is possible. A 2,500-word SIGGRAPH Asia short paper and a 12-page DIS Pictorial are not the same paper at different lengths — they are different arguments. A Pictorial makes its case *visually*, with text supporting images; a short paper makes its case in prose, with images as evidence. Deciding this in session 12 means rewriting everything.

Some venues also require the artefact to be *exhibited*, with technical riders, shipping and install. That is a production commitment, and it has to be planned from the middle of the semester.

Read the venue's **review form** as soon as you choose. It tells you literally what the reviewers are instructed to look for. You will use it yourself in the session 12 mock review.

## 9. Ways this goes wrong

**The chronicle.** "First I did X, then Y, then Z." Activity is not findings. Every sentence about what you did should be earning its place in an argument.

**The artist statement in disguise.** Evocative, first-person, unfalsifiable. Fine in a gallery; not a paper.

**Unsupported novelty.** "No one has done this before." Prove it with a related-work section, or drop the claim. Reviewers check.

**Interest mistaken for finding.** "I found this fascinating" is about you. "This produced an unexpected X, which suggests Y" is about the world.

**The technology demo.** The paper explains how a system works and stops. So what? A tool description is not a contribution unless something is learned from building or using it.

**Post-hoc rationalisation.** Pretending you planned what you stumbled into. Reviewers in this field are practitioners and can smell it. **The honest version is stronger**: an accurately reported stumble, analysed for why it happened, is a real finding. A fabricated clean narrative is a weak paper *and* a research-integrity problem.

**Documentation left to the end.** Unrecoverable. See §5.

## 10. AI assistance, declared properly

You are required to use Cursor, and your code will be substantially AI-assisted. This is a legitimate method, and it needs handling openly.

**In the course:** every submission carries a short verbal walkthrough in which you explain a section of code you did not write yourself. If you cannot explain it, it does not count as yours. This is not a trap — it is the actual skill the course teaches, which is reading and judging systems rather than typing them.

**In a paper:** most venues now require disclosure of generative AI use, and the policies differ — check your target venue's specific statement before submitting. General principles that hold across venues:

- AI tools are **not** authors and cannot be listed as such.
- You are fully responsible for everything in the submission, including anything a model produced.
- Disclose AI use in a methods or acknowledgements statement — what tool, for what purpose.
- Using an LLM to *generate code that you then evaluate and modify* is ordinarily fine and is worth describing as part of your method. Using one to *write the paper's argument* is not.

There is a genuine research angle here, and it is under-explored: **AI-assisted implementation changes what a designer can attempt, and therefore changes the practice.** If your journal records how working with a coding assistant altered your process — what you attempted that you would not have, where it misled you, what you stopped learning — that is a publishable observation in its own right, and one the field currently wants.

## 11. Ethics, briefly

- **Human participants** — if anyone will be observed, interviewed, or studied using your work, you need approval *before* you collect anything. Start by session 5 or choose a contribution type that does not require it.
- **Documentation of people** — consent for any recognisable person in photographs or video, including at the exhibition.
- **Site and heritage work** — permission for scanning or recording in private or protected locations. Relevant to anyone working with Macau's built environment.
- **Datasets and models** — know the licence of anything you train on or generate from, and say so.
- **Attribution** — if your process builds on someone's published technique, cite it. Practice communities are small and reviewers often *are* the person you borrowed from.

## 13. Checklist before you submit

- [ ] Can I state my contribution in one sentence, without describing the artefact?
- [ ] Is my claim contestable — could a reasonable person disagree?
- [ ] Does the claim extend beyond my own project?
- [ ] Is every claim evidenced from documentation or journal, not memory?
- [ ] Does the related-work section build the gap, rather than list what I read?
- [ ] Have I explained my *method* — how I worked and how I analysed what I recorded?
- [ ] Is the documentation legible as evidence, at consistent conditions, with scale?
- [ ] Have I stated limitations?
- [ ] Does it follow the venue's template, word limit, and anonymisation rules exactly?
- [ ] Have I disclosed AI assistance per the venue's policy?
- [ ] Have I checked it against the venue's own review form?
