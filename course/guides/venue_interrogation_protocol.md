**ARTD8105 · v1.0 · 16 August 2026**
*How to reconstruct a venue's standards from public evidence, and encode them so the agent can reason from them.*

---

## Why this exists

In a competition, the standard is published. Judging criteria, a scoring scale, past winners with stated reasons. You can read what "good" means.

Academic venues do not work that way. Accepted papers are public; **why they were accepted is not.** The standard lives in reviewers' heads, in editorial habit, in what a community treats as too obvious to state. From outside, it is invisible.

That invisibility is not evenly distributed. Researchers trained inside a venue's community absorbed the standard by osmosis — through supervisors who reviewed for it, labmates who published in it, corridor conversations after rejections. If you did not have that, you are being assessed against a standard nobody wrote down for you.

**This protocol is how you write it down for yourself.** It is a method for reconstructing tacit professional standards from public artifacts. It is also, if you want it, a research contribution in its own right — the reconstruction problem is real and under-described.

Run it once per candidate venue. It takes about three hours the first time and forty minutes thereafter.

---

## What you produce

A file at `venue/<venue-slug>.md` — for example `venue/eva-london-2027.md`. The agent reads it and reasons from it explicitly. Without it, the agent is guessing from averaged impressions of academic norms that are dated and skewed toward large computer-science conferences. With it, the agent's judgements are accountable to something you can check.

You will build two or three of these in Step 4, then one becomes primary in Step 6.

---

## Phase 1 — Harvest the declared standard

Everything a venue says about itself, in its own words. Collect verbatim; do not paraphrase yet.

**Find and record:**

1. **Call for papers** — full text, including the theme if there is one
2. **Article or track types** — the full list, with word limits and formats. *This matters more than anything else in Phase 1.* Venues that look inhospitable often have one track built for exactly your kind of work.
3. **Author guidelines** — length, template, figure limits, reference style, supplementary material policy
4. **The review form or review criteria** — see below
5. **Anonymisation requirements** — blind, double-blind, or open
6. **AI use policy** — current and venue-specific; do not assume
7. **Ethics requirements** — human participants, image consent, data availability
8. **Deadlines** — abstract, full paper, notification, camera-ready
9. **Cost** — APC, registration, whether a hybrid or online option exists
10. **Indexing** — ACM DL, Scopus, EI, Web of Science index letter

### The review form is the prize

**Ask the agent to search for the venue's reviewer instructions specifically.** They are often published and rarely read by authors. Search terms that work:

- `"<venue> reviewer guidelines"`
- `"<venue> review form"`
- `"<venue>" "instructions for reviewers"`
- `"<venue> AC guidelines"` or `"associate chair"`
- `site:<venue-domain> review`

If the form is not published, look for:
- A **program chairs' blog post or editorial** about what they are looking for — these often state the standard more plainly than any official document
- A **"how to review for us"** page, sometimes buried in a volunteer or mentoring section
- The **rebuttal instructions**, which reveal what reviewers are asked to score
- Meta-reviews or public reviews, where the venue uses an open review platform

Record it verbatim. If you genuinely cannot find one, say so in the file — an absent review form is itself information about how the venue operates, and it means Phase 3 carries more weight.

---

## Phase 2 — Harvest exemplars

**Select three to five accepted papers**, all from your intended article type, all from the last three years.

Choose deliberately, not by convenience:

- At least one that is **structurally similar to your project** — same contribution type, comparable scope
- At least one that is **as far from yours as the venue allows** — this shows you the width of the box
- At least one **award-winning or best-paper** if the venue has such a thing, since that is the closest a venue comes to publishing its own taste
- Avoid papers by famous authors — they get accepted on things you cannot replicate

Get the full text. If a paper is paywalled, check the authors' own pages, institutional repositories, or the ACM Author-Izer link; most are available. The UM library has ACM DL access.

---

## Phase 3 — Derive the implicit standard

This is the analytical core, and the part the agent is genuinely good at. **Read for form, not content.**

For each exemplar, have the agent extract and tabulate:

| Dimension | What to measure |
|---|---|
| **Total length** | Words excluding references |
| **Section proportions** | Word count per section, as a percentage |
| **Where the claim first appears** | Paragraph number in the introduction |
| **How the claim is worded** | Verbatim — the actual contribution sentence |
| **Artifact:analysis ratio** | Words describing what was made vs. words analysing what it revealed |
| **What related work does** | Is it a list, or does it build a gap? How many citations? |
| **How process is evidenced** | Journal quotes? Images? Numbers? Nothing? |
| **Figure count and type** | Photographs, diagrams, screenshots, tables |
| **Whether limitations appear** | And how much space they get |
| **First-person or not** | "We made" vs. "the system was built" |
| **Tense** | Present or past for describing the work |

Then ask the agent for the **cross-exemplar pattern**: what do all of these do that a rejected paper probably would not? Where do they differ from each other, and what does that variation tell you about the width of the standard?

The output of Phase 3 is a set of statements like:

> *All three papers state the contribution within the first three paragraphs, in a sentence beginning "We contribute" or "This paper contributes." Description of the artifact averages 22% of total words; analysis of what it revealed averages 34%. All three quote from a process record. None uses the passive voice for their own actions.*

That is a standard. You derived it. It is checkable.

## Phase 4 — Write the venue file

Use `course/templates/venue_file.md`. The structure:

```markdown
---
venue: EVA London 2027
slug: eva-london-2027
type: conference
track: Short Paper
derived: 2026-09-24
derived_by: <student name>
exemplars_read: 4
review_form_found: true
---

## Declared standard
[Phase 1, verbatim where it matters]

### Hard constraints
- Length:
- Template:
- Anonymisation:
- AI policy:
- Deadline:
- Cost:
- Indexing:

### Review criteria (verbatim)
[the review form, or a note that none was found and what you used instead]

## Derived standard
[Phase 3 findings, as checkable statements]

### Section proportions observed
| Section | Exemplar A | B | C | Mean |

### Contribution sentences, verbatim
1. "..."  — Author, Year
2. "..."  — Author, Year

### What this venue appears to reward
### What this venue appears to punish
### Width of the box — where the exemplars differ

## Validation
[Phase 5 result]

## Open questions about this venue
[what you could not determine]
```

---

## Phase 5 — Validate the derivation

**Do not skip this.** It is what makes the file a reconstruction rather than a guess.

Take one exemplar you did **not** use in Phase 3. Score it against the standard you just derived, as if you were reviewing it.

- **If it passes comfortably** — your derivation is probably sound.
- **If it fails** — your derivation is wrong, because that paper was accepted. Go back to Phase 3 and find what you missed. Usually you have over-fitted to a surface feature or imported a norm from a different venue.

Record the result in the file, including a failed first attempt if you had one. A venue file that shows a corrected derivation is more trustworthy than one that claims first-time success.

---

## Phase 6 — Keep it live

The venue file is not finished when you write it.

- **After the mock review (Step 10)**, add what your reviewer noticed that your file did not predict.
- **After any real review**, add what the reviewers actually rewarded and punished. This is the highest-quality evidence you will ever get about the venue, and almost nobody records it.
- **Before resubmission anywhere**, re-derive. Standards drift and chairs rotate.

---

## Using the file with the agent

Once `venue/<slug>.md` exists:

```
@venue/eva-london-2027.md

Score my introduction against the derived standard in the venue file.
Name each criterion you apply and quote the file. Where the file is
silent, say so rather than filling the gap from general impressions.
```

Good agent behaviour looks like:

> Applying "contribution stated within first three paragraphs" — yours appears in paragraph 6. Applying "artifact description ≤ 25% of total" — yours is 41%, so 610 words over. The file is silent on figure captions, so I have no venue-specific basis for judging yours.

Bad agent behaviour looks like: *"This is a strong introduction that clearly conveys your contribution."* If you get that, ask it to name criteria and quote the file.

---

## Honest limits

Say these out loud in your paper if the method is part of your contribution.

**A derived standard is a reconstruction, not the thing itself.** You are inferring a decision process from its outputs. Accepted papers tell you what passed; they cannot tell you what was rejected, and the rejected set is where the boundary actually is.

**Survivorship bias is structural and unfixable from outside.** Every exemplar you can read was accepted. You are reading the survivors.

**Reviewer variance is large.** Two reviewers at the same venue disagree routinely. A derived standard describes a central tendency, not a decision rule.

**Award-winners are atypical by definition.** Useful for reading a venue's aspiration; misleading for reading its floor.

**You may over-fit to surface features.** Section proportions are easy to measure and may not be what reviewers care about. Phase 5 catches the worst of this; it does not eliminate it.

None of this makes the exercise pointless. An explicit, checkable, wrong-in-known-ways reconstruction is better than an implicit, uncheckable impression — which is what you have otherwise.
