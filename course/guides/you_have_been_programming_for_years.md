## 1. What you already do

You use Photoshop. Illustrator. InDesign. After Effects. When you open one of these you see panels and sliders and tools.

Underneath, all of it is instructions written by people — code that tells the computer what to do with every pixel. Every one of these programs was built by a team who decided what it should let you do and what it should make impossible. Each has its own **affordances**: the things it makes easy, and the things it makes unthinkable.

The loop is always the same. You **input** a command by clicking or pressing a key. The software **processes** it by executing an algorithm. The **output** changes — a line appears, a colour shifts, a shape transforms.

So: every time you click your mouse, tap your screen, or use a keyboard shortcut, **you are executing code.**

When you learn a piece of software, what you are actually learning is a sequence of operations that reliably produces a result. You take the idea in your head and translate it into a series of actions — select this, adjust that, apply this, undo, try again, group, export. That translation is an algorithm. Those actions are instructions.

By learning a piece of software, you become part of the program.

Most of you would not call yourselves programmers. You have been programming for years. What you have not been doing is **writing the instructions down somewhere another person could read them.** That distinction turns out to matter enormously, and it is what most of this document is about.

## 2. The material underneath

One more thing before we move on, because it is the reason you should not be frightened of Blender in week five.

Underneath every tool you have ever used are three kinds of thing. **Raster images** are grids of coloured squares — numbers. **Vectors** are shapes defined by mathematical formulas and coordinates. **3D meshes** are vertices and polygons in coordinate space.

That is the material. All of it.

Once you see that, every design program starts to look similar, because you are always learning a new interface onto the same pixels and bits. You are never starting from zero when you open unfamiliar software. You are learning a new set of buttons onto material you already understand.

Doctors learn organic chemistry even though they spend their working lives in physiology. The principle is the same: **learn the medium, not only the tool.**

## 3. Three kinds of programming

The AI researcher Andrej Karpathy has a taxonomy that is worth knowing.

**Software 1.0 — explicit code.** A human writes every instruction. If X, then Y. You can read it, trace it, and know exactly what will happen. Almost everything you used before about three years ago is Software 1.0.

**Software 2.0 — neural weights.** Instead of writing the rules, we define a goal and supply data, and the system finds the rules itself. Think of a rat learning a maze: it fails many times, then it stops failing. The maze is the neural network. This is enormously powerful and it is a black box — nobody can read the weights and tell you *why* the model produced what it did.

**Software 3.0 — natural language.** The code is English. Or Chinese, or Portuguese. You describe what you want and the system produces it.

Notice who does the programming in each case. Software 1.0 was written by computer scientists. Software 2.0 by data scientists. **Software 3.0 is written by anyone who can speak.**

The barrier to entry moved from learning syntax to speaking precisely.

## 4. The turn

Here is the consequence, and it is the reason this course is shaped the way it is.

**If the code is now language, then writing precisely is the technical skill.**

This is not a metaphor, and it is not a consolation prize for people who would rather not learn to program. If the instruction *is* the program, then the quality of your instruction determines the quality of your result — entirely. A vague instruction produces a vague artifact. This is true whether the executor is a compiler, a diffusion model, an assistant, or a person with a pencil.

You already know a version of this from image generation. "Blue" is not enough — cobalt, cerulean, indigo and electric blue produce four different images. And to get photographic results you have to speak photography: aperture, bokeh, depth of field. To get a particular quality of light you need subsurface scattering, ambient occlusion, volumetric fog.

Vocabulary is not decoration. **Vocabulary is the instrument.**

Now hold that standard and point it at a research claim, because the same thing is true there and the stakes are higher.

## 5. 1968

Go backwards fifty-seven years.

**Sol LeWitt** made wall drawings and did not draw them. He wrote instructions; other people executed them — draftsmen, technicians, students. Many were executed after he died. Every execution is different. Every one is authentic. When a museum acquires a LeWitt wall drawing, what it acquires is a **certificate**: a piece of paper carrying the instruction, and the right to have it made.

That is source code. Written in English. Executed by a human interpreter. In 1968. Software 3.0, fifty-seven years early.

Four years before that, **Yoko Ono** published *Grapefruit*, a book of instructions. Some are executable. Some are not. The unexecutable ones are the interesting ones, because they show that an instruction can do real work even when it cannot be carried out.

Two questions come out of this, and they run through the whole course:

- What happens when an instruction **cannot** be executed?
- What happens when two people execute **the same instruction** and get different results?

## 6. An instruction is a method

Read a LeWitt certificate the way a scientist reads a paper.

It is a **protocol** — a written procedure, specified precisely enough that someone else can carry it out.

It is **executed by another party** — the author is not present, so the document must carry everything necessary. Anything left in the author's head is simply lost.

It is **variable but valid** — every execution differs, none is wrong, and the work is really the *space* of possible outcomes rather than any single one.

Protocol. Independently executed. Variable but valid.

That is not only a description of conceptual art. **It is a description of a research method.**

Which gives us the sentence this course rests on:

> **Writing an instruction is writing a method.**

You already know how to do the hardest part of research. You have done it every time you worked out a repeatable process and taught it to someone else. What you have not done is write it down so a stranger could reproduce it, and then reason carefully about what the results tell you.

That is the gap. It is smaller than it looks, and closing it is what this term is for.

## 7. Three kinds of research

One piece of vocabulary, from Christopher Frayling in 1993. You will meet it again in week two.

**Research *into* art and design** — historical, theoretical, critical. You study objects, movements, documents. This is a real and rigorous tradition and it is broadly what you were taught in your first year.

**Research *through* art and design** — the making *is* the inquiry. You produce something in order to find something out, and the knowledge emerges from the process and the object together.

**Research *for* art and design** — you gather knowledge in order to make a better thing. The endpoint is the artifact itself.

Almost every designer's instinct is research *for*. It is what professional practice is: you research the client, the market, the material, so the product works. It is also the one that does not publish, because the knowledge stays with you.

A Doctor of Design lives in research *through*. The test is simple:

> **Does someone who never sees your object gain something usable from your account of making it?**

If yes, you have research. If no, you have a project.

There is a real adjustment here. In the tradition you were trained in, the evidence already exists and you interpret it. **In practice-led research you make the object you then reason from.** The evidence does not exist until you produce it. That changes what counts as rigour, what counts as data, and — crucially — what you have to write down *while you work*, because you cannot reconstruct it afterwards.

## 8. "I made this" is not a claim

A portfolio shows **what you made**. A paper argues **what is now known that was not known before**, in a form that lets someone else use it.

The gap between them is not quality of execution. Your craft is real. But a beautiful object with no claim attached is not research, and a modest object with a clear, evidenced, transferable claim is.

### The four sentences

Write these about your own project in week five. They will be bad. Rewrite them every few weeks and keep every version — how your claim changes as you make things is itself one of the most publishable things you will have.

1. **I made** ——— *(the artifact, described factually)*
2. **In making it I found that** ——— *(the observation — something that surprised you, resisted you, or turned out otherwise than expected)*
3. **Which means that** ——— *(the claim — a general statement extending beyond your object)*
4. **Which is useful to** ——— *(who can act on it, and how)*

Sentences 1, 2 and 4 come easily to designers. **Sentence 3 is where papers are won and lost**, because it requires saying something true of more than your own project, and defending it.

### Weak

> *"I made a generative type system in p5.js that produces posters from a rule set. The results were visually interesting and I explored many variations."*

Nothing here is a claim. "Visually interesting" is taste — a reader has no way to agree or disagree. "Explored many variations" is an activity, and activity is not a finding.

### Stronger

> *"I made a generative type system in p5.js in which the rule set is authored but the letterform outcomes are not previewable. Working with it, I found that my editorial judgement shifted from selecting outcomes to constraining the space of outcomes — and that I could not evaluate a rule set without generating at least ~40 instances. This suggests that generative authorship relocates the designer's critical act from* choosing *to* bounding*, and that tools for generative typography should prioritise rapid population-level preview over single-instance refinement. That is actionable for anyone building or teaching with such tools."*

Same project. What changed: one observation was isolated, generalised into a claim about generative authorship, and aimed at someone who could act on it.

Notice the number. Forty instances is a specific, checkable detail that came out of a process record, and it is exactly the kind of thing reviewers trust. **Specificity is what separates a claim from an opinion.**

Notice also that you could disagree with it. Someone could run the same experiment and need fifteen. **That is a feature.** A claim nobody could dispute is usually not a claim — it is a description in a claim's clothing. If nothing could falsify it, it is not doing any work.

## 9. What this means for how you work from Monday

Three practical consequences, all of which start immediately.

**Record while you work, not afterwards.** Memory smooths. It removes the false starts and tidies the sequence into something that looks planned. Those false starts are the evidence. Your process journal and your agent log both begin in week one for this reason, and neither can be reconstructed later.

**Failures are your most valuable material.** A failed print, a rule set that produced nothing, an approach that collapsed — these reveal the constraint structure of a material or a tool, which is precisely the kind of knowledge that transfers to someone who never sees your object. A term with no recorded failures is a term with no findings.

**Rejection is a skill you are graded on.** The agent you work with is fluent, confident, and often wrong. Students who accept its first plausible suggestion produce work that is competent, generic and unpublishable. What matters is turning things down and knowing why — and recording it at the moment, not remembering it in November.
