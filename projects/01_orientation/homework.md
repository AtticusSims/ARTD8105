## 4 · Read two papers, and write one reading note — **by hand**

**Everybody reads Frayling.** *Research in Art and Design*, Royal College of Art Research Papers 1(1), 1993/4, five pages, free from the RCA repository — link in the readings guide.

**Then one paper of your own**, from the venue that matches the kind of designer you are. The table is on slide 51 of the session 1 deck and on the Notion page. Three rules: published in the last five years, taken from the named venue rather than from a search result, and full text you can actually open.

Write **one reading note** on *your own* paper — not on Frayling — using `course/templates/reading_note.md`. Sections 1 through 4 and 7 only; skip *Form* and *Venue signal* for now, we come to those in session 6. About one page, about forty minutes. These are working notes, not essays.

> ### This first note is written without AI
>
> No Cursor, no chatbot, no summariser. The paper, the template, and you.
>
> There are two reasons. The practical one is that most of you will still be wrestling with an installer this weekend, and I would rather that time went into reading. The real one is that **you cannot supervise a tool at a task you have never performed yourself.** From session 3 onwards you will point the agent at papers and have it help you measure a paper's *form*, and the only thing that will let you catch it being wrong is knowing what a good note feels like from the inside.
>
> One by hand, first. After that the tools are open — with one permanent exception: **never ask an agent to write section 3, the critical thinking section.** If you outsource your critique you have outsourced the only part of reading that matters.

If your setup is not finished, write the note on paper and transcribe it later. Do not let the tooling stop you reading.

## 5 · Process journal entry #1 — **tonight**

Write it **within twenty-four hours of class**, while you can still remember what you expected. Memory smooths: it tidies the false starts out of the sequence, and the false starts are the evidence.

Copy `course/templates/process_journal_entry.md` into `process_journal/` in your repository, named by date. Answer these eight questions about today's workshop:

1. What did you expect to get back? Attach the slip you folded before handing over version one.
2. What did you actually get? Photograph it.
3. Where exactly did the two diverge? Name the words responsible.
4. Which parts of your instruction could have gone several ways without changing anything you cared about? **(the boring region)**
5. Where did the execution stop looking like what you pictured — but still count as a valid reading of what you wrote? **(the surprising region)**
6. What would have made a result *not* a valid execution at all? **(the breaking point)**
7. What did you change in version two, and what did you predict the change would do?
8. Did it do that? If not, why not — a guess is fine, but say that it is a guess.

Those three phrases in bold come back next Wednesday with a slider attached. Learn them here, where everything is slow enough to watch.

The journal is graded under the project component. It is a data collection instrument and you are the subject — not a diary and not a progress report.

## 6 · One page: where you are starting from

Not assessed. Genuinely useful.

Write roughly one page covering:

- **What you want from this course.** Be honest, including if the answer is "I am not sure yet."
- **What you think your research interest is** — even if it is vague, even if it changes completely by October. Most of them do. If you already have a research proposal from your application, tell me what it says.
- **What you can currently do technically.** Software you know, whether you have written code before, whether you have used a 3D program, whether you have made anything physical. There is no wrong answer and no expectation — I need to know where to pitch the technical sessions.
- **Anything you are worried about.**

Bring it printed, or email it to me before class.
